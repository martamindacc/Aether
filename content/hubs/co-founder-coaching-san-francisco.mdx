## If a deadlock turns legal in California

Most founding teams never take a deadlock to court. If yours is heading there, these are the California rules founders most often meet.

- **A California corporation.** Under [Corporations Code § 1800](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_1800), shareholders holding at least a third of the shares can ask a court to dissolve the company when the board is evenly split and cannot agree, or when factions of shareholders are so deadlocked that the business can no longer be run to their advantage. The other shareholders can usually avoid dissolution by buying out the petitioner at a fair value the court sets.
- **When one co-founder leaves.** California voids most non-compete agreements ([Business and Professions Code § 16600](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_16600)), and since 2024 the ban reaches even narrowly drawn clauses. A co-founder who walks away can often start a competitor the next week. Narrow exceptions exist, such as selling the business.
- **A Delaware corporation**, as most venture-backed Bay Area startups are. Under [Section 226](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc07/index.html) of the Delaware General Corporation Law, the Court of Chancery can appoint a custodian when stockholders or directors are so divided that the company cannot elect a board or act. Dissolution on deadlock is narrower still.

All of these routes are slow, public and blunt. They settle who keeps the company, not how the two of you work together. This is general information, not legal advice: for your own situation, talk to California counsel.
