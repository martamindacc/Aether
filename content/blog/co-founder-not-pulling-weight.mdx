*This article is part of our guide to [co-founder conflict](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide).*

It started as something you noticed and dismissed. Then it was something you worked around. Now you plan your week on the assumption that the thing you asked for will not arrive, and you have stopped asking, because asking and not receiving is worse than not asking.

You have not said anything. Twelve months, in some cases two years, and you have not said anything — because what exactly do you say to the person who quit a job and told their family it would be fine, who sat with you in the bad months, and who owns half of this?

## Short answer: what do you do about a co-founder who has stopped delivering?

You name it early, specifically, and in private — and you do it long before you are certain, because by the time you are certain you will be too angry to do it well.

The mistake almost every founder makes is waiting for enough evidence to be unarguable. By then you have a year of accumulated resentment and a case to prosecute, and you will deliver it as a verdict. Your co-founder, who has been living in a different version of the same year, will experience it as an ambush. Then you are not solving a contribution problem, you are managing the aftermath of one.

The conversation is far easier at month two than month twenty, and the only reason nobody has it at month two is that at month two you are not yet sure.

Have it anyway. "I'm not sure, and I want to check" is a conversation. "I've concluded" is a sentence.

## First, check whether you are measuring the right thing

Before anything else, test your own read. Founders are unreliable narrators about contribution, systematically and in a predictable direction.

**You see all of your own work and a fraction of theirs.** You know every hour you put in, every call you took, every thing you worried about at 2am. Of their work, you see the output and almost none of the effort. This asymmetry alone produces a persistent sense that you are carrying more — in both founders, simultaneously.

**You value the kind of work you do.** A technical founder counts shipped code. A commercial founder counts closed revenue. Each is likely to under-weight what the other does, particularly the invisible work: the partnership that took four months of relationship-building, the disaster that was quietly avoided and therefore never happened.

**Visible effort is not contribution.** The founder who is always in the office at 9pm may be producing less than the one who leaves at six and makes two decisive calls a week. Hours are the worst available proxy and the most tempting one.

So before you raise it, write down specifically what you believe is not happening. Not "they're not pulling their weight" — that is a feeling. **"The hiring plan has been owned by them since March and we have made two of the six hires, and I have run four of those processes myself."** That is a fact, and facts are discussable.

If you cannot produce three specific items, you may have a resentment problem rather than a contribution problem, and the conversation you need is a different one. It is also worth checking whether what you are calling a contribution gap is really [a decision-rights gap](/blog/co-founder-5050-deadlock): a founder who has been overruled on everything in their domain for a year often looks, from outside, like a founder who has stopped trying.

## Four different problems that look identical from outside

**A co-founder who has stopped delivering is almost always one of four things — burnout, mismatch, disengagement or quiet departure — and we call these the Four Readings because you cannot tell them apart from outside; you can only read them by asking.** This is the part that matters most, because the response to each is completely different.

### 1. Burnout

They are not refusing to work. They cannot. The capacity is gone, usually after a long period of over-extension that you both treated as normal. Output drops, decisions get avoided, the easy tasks get done because the hard ones require a reserve that is empty.

The tell: it is a change. There was a version of this person who delivered, often spectacularly, and the decline has a rough start date.

**This is the most common of the four and the most frequently misread as laziness.** It also responds to rest, reduced scope and support — which means treating it as a performance problem makes it worse and can cost you a co-founder who would otherwise have recovered.

### 2. Mismatch

They were superb at the company you had and are struggling with the company you have. The founder who thrived at eight people is drowning at forty. The brilliant builder is now expected to manage, and hates it.

The tell: they are still working hard. The output is just no longer landing, and they often know it before you do.

This is not a character problem and it is frequently solvable by changing the role rather than the person.

### 3. Disengagement

They have stopped believing. In the strategy, in the market, in your leadership, in the outcome. They have not said so, possibly not even to themselves, but their effort has already followed their conviction.

The tell: selective energy. Flat on the core business, suddenly alive about a side project or a conference or anything that is not this.

### 4. Quiet departure

They have already left, internally. They are waiting for a vesting date, a funding event, or an exit that lets them go without looking like the person who quit.

The tell: the minimum, consistently, with no distress about it. Burnout is uncomfortable for the person experiencing it. Quiet departure is comfortable.

**You cannot tell these apart from the outside. All four look like someone doing less.** The only way to find out is to ask, and asking requires a conversation that assumes you do not already know the answer.

## Why founders wait far too long

Naming the real reasons tends to shorten the wait.

**You are afraid of the answer.** If they say "yes, I'm done," you are running the company alone, and some part of you would rather have a half-present co-founder than confront that.

**You feel disloyal.** They were there in the bad months. Raising this feels like a betrayal of that history.

**The equity makes it enormous.** This is not a performance conversation. It is a conversation that sits one step away from the ownership of the company, and you both know it, which makes it almost impossible to start lightly.

**You are not certain, and you want to be fair.** The most honourable reason, and the most expensive. Waiting for certainty guarantees you will raise it angry.

Meanwhile the cost compounds. Your resentment is rising, their awareness that something is wrong is rising, and the team has almost certainly noticed — teams always notice, far earlier than founders believe, and they draw their own conclusions about which founder actually runs the company.

## How to have the conversation

**Do it in person, with time, nowhere near the office.** This conversation needs ninety minutes and no possibility of being overheard or interrupted. If you are in different cities, a long video call with cameras on is better than waiting another month for a trip.

**Say what it is at the start.** "I want to talk about how the work is split. I've been sitting on this too long." Do not lead with warmth and swerve into it — your co-founder will spend the rest of the conversation re-examining the opening.

**Open with observation, not conclusion.** "Here's what I've seen over the last six months" — then the three specific things. Stop. Let them respond before you add interpretation.

**Then ask the question you do not know the answer to.** "What's going on for you?" And then be quiet. This is the most important thirty seconds in the conversation and the one founders most often fill.

**Be prepared for a ledger in return.** There is a reasonable chance they have their own account of the last year in which you are the problem — you took over their domain, you stopped trusting them, you made them irrelevant and are now blaming them for being irrelevant. That account may be partly true. If you cannot hear it, the conversation will not work.

**Do not arrive at an outcome in the first conversation.** Diagnosis first. Decisions later, in a second conversation, once both of you have slept on it.

## What a real agreement looks like afterwards

Vague resolutions are how this problem reaches year two. If the conversation goes well, it ends with something specific:

- **What they own**, explicitly, and what they do not own any more
- **What "working" looks like**, in outcomes, in ninety days
- **A date** to review it — in the calendar before you leave the room
- **What changes on your side.** There is almost always something. If the answer is nothing, you were not listening.

If it was burnout, the agreement is about reduced scope and actual recovery time, not a promise to try harder. If it was mismatch, the agreement is a different role. If it was disengagement or quiet departure, the honest agreement is about a transition — and a planned, dignified exit is vastly better for the company than eighteen more months of this.

## If nothing changes

If you have had the conversation properly, agreed something specific, given it a real ninety days, and nothing has moved, then you are no longer dealing with a contribution problem. You are dealing with a partnership that one person is no longer in.

The options narrow to three.

**Change the role permanently.** They step back from operating into something smaller and genuinely defined — advisor, board seat, a specific function. This works when there is goodwill, and it requires honesty about title and equity rather than a polite fiction that fools nobody, least of all the team.

**Negotiate an exit.** Hard, and frequently the right answer. [Separating well is its own skill](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit), and a founding team that does it with care protects the company. One that does it badly can take the company down with the relationship.

**Accept it.** A real option, rarely named. You decide the company is worth more to you than the fairness of the split, and you stop keeping the ledger. The crucial word is *stop* — accepting while continuing to resent is the worst of all available outcomes, and it is where most teams actually end up.

Underneath all three sits the vesting question. If there is no vesting, a co-founder who leaves — or stays and does nothing — holds a large stake in a company they are not building. This is why vesting exists and why adding it is usually the first structural move for any founding team that does not have it. If the real grievance is that the cap table no longer reflects who built the company, [renegotiating co-founder equity](/blog/renegotiating-co-founder-equity) is a separate conversation with its own sequence, and it goes better after this one than instead of it.

## If you are the one being told

Worth including, because half the people reading this are on the other side of it.

**The feedback is probably not wholly wrong.** Something has changed, or this conversation would not be happening. Resist the first instinct, which is to produce counter-evidence.

**Find out which of the four it is for yourself.** Are you exhausted, in the wrong role, no longer convinced, or already gone? Nobody can answer that but you, and until you do, you cannot have an honest conversation about it.

**If you are burned out, say so.** Plainly. The thing you are afraid of — that admitting it costs you standing — is far less costly than letting it be read as not caring, which is how it is currently landing.

**If you have checked out, consider saying that too.** A founder who names their own departure and manages it well leaves with a reputation intact and a company that survives. A founder who waits to be pushed does not get either.

## FAQ: co-founder underperformance

### How long should I wait before raising it with my co-founder?

Far less time than you think. Weeks, not quarters. Raise it as an observation while you are still genuinely unsure — "I've noticed something and I want to check" is an easy conversation that becomes impossible once you have concluded.

### How do I tell whether my co-founder is burned out or has checked out?

You cannot tell from the outside, which is why you have to ask. Burnout is a change with a rough start date and is uncomfortable for the person living it; they usually want to recover and will engage with a plan that has a review date. Quiet departure is comfortable: consistent minimum effort with no distress, and resistance to any plan because a plan has a deadline.

### Can you fire a co-founder?

Legally it depends entirely on your structure — board composition, voting rights, vesting, your shareholders' agreement — and it is a question for a startup lawyer, not an article. Practically: removing a co-founder who holds a large stake and will not go willingly is slow, expensive and damaging, which is precisely why the conversation is worth having long before it gets there.

### What if the whole team can see that one founder is not contributing?

They can. They saw it before you raised it, and the longer it runs unaddressed, the more the team concludes that performance has no consequences at the top. That is a culture cost separate from the contribution cost.

### Should I talk to our investors first?

No. Investors are not neutral and their obligation is to the company. Once a board member knows, the situation has a constituency and your co-founder will find out it was discussed without them, which ends any chance of repair.

### My co-founder says they are burned out. Is that just an excuse?

Sometimes it is cover, usually it is not. The distinguishing question is whether they want to recover. Someone burned out but still invested will engage with reduced scope and a plan. Someone using it as cover will resist any plan, because a plan has a review date.

### Should we get outside help for this conversation?

If you have been rehearsing it alone for months, yes. The version in your head has become a prosecution, and a neutral third person in the room makes it possible for your account and theirs to both exist without one having to be wrong. [Co-founder coaching, mediation and counsel each fix different things](/blog/co-founder-mediation-vs-coaching); for this conversation, coaching is usually the right first call.
