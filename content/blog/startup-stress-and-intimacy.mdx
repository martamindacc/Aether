In the first year of the company, you still had sex. Stressed, tired, but connected, and the shared mission gave the relationship a charge. By year three it had become rare. By year four, one of you had stopped initiating because the rejection hurt too much, and the other had started avoiding the goodnight kiss because it might lead somewhere they had no energy for.

Nobody talks about this part of founder life. The podcasts cover burnout, fundraising, hiring. They don't cover what it does to a couple when the body of one partner is permanently in a conference room and the body of the other has been running the household alone for so long that being touched feels like one more demand.

## Short answer: Why does sex disappear during a startup?

Three things, stacking.

**Biology.** Chronic stress elevates cortisol and keeps the nervous system in threat mode. In that state, the body deprioritises everything not needed for survival, including desire. This is not a choice or a character flaw. It's physiology.

**Distance.** For most people in long relationships, desire follows closeness rather than preceding it. When the relationship has become logistical, there is no path to desire, because the thing it grows from has gone.

**Pattern.** Once sex becomes rare, a dynamic forms: one partner initiates and is declined; the hurt accumulates; they stop initiating. The other partner feels the pressure, starts avoiding affection in general to prevent it leading anywhere, and the gap widens. That pattern persists long after the stress that caused it has passed.

The fix is not scheduling. It is addressing all three.

## What chronic stress does to desire

Acute stress can heighten arousal. Chronic stress does the opposite, and founders live in chronic.

Under sustained stress:
- Cortisol stays elevated, suppressing sex hormones
- The nervous system stays in sympathetic activation (fight-or-flight), which is incompatible with the parasympathetic state arousal needs
- Sleep is disrupted, and sleep deprivation independently reduces desire
- Attention narrows to threat, and a partner's touch doesn't register as pleasure; it registers as input
- The body prioritises recovery, and sex is energetically expensive

A founder in a fundraise, a co-founder dispute, or a near-death cash moment is not biologically available for intimacy. Telling them to "prioritise the relationship" is like telling someone with the flu to go for a run.

The partner may be in a similar state for different reasons: solo parenting, financial fear, chronic over-functioning. Two bodies in threat mode do not reach for each other.

## The founder's body is not at home

A specific founder phenomenon: physical presence with total mental absence.

The founder is in bed, but their mind is in tomorrow's board meeting. Their body is tense, their attention is elsewhere, and if touched, their first response is often a flinch or a sigh, not from rejection but from interruption.

Partners experience this as being invisible. They reach for someone who is there and not there, and the reach fails. Over time they stop reaching, not because desire is gone but because reaching for an absent body is humiliating.

Founders rarely notice this. From the inside, they're home. They don't experience their own absence.

## The partner's desire, and why distance kills it

The partner's side is different and equally structural.

Most partners of founders are over-functioning: running the household, often parenting mostly alone, holding the emotional thermostat. They are tired in a way that isn't just physical. And they are often emotionally distant from a partner who has become a sealed, absent, occasionally discharging presence.

For most people, especially in long relationships, desire needs two things: capacity (rest, headspace) and connection (feeling close, seen, wanted as a person). The partner of a founder frequently has neither. Being asked for sex in that state feels like being asked for one more thing by someone who hasn't asked how you are in weeks.

This is why "he/she never wants to anymore" is almost never about attraction. It's about depletion and distance. Fix those, and desire often returns on its own.

## Spontaneous vs responsive desire: the model stressed couples need

This single idea reframes the problem for most couples.

**Spontaneous desire** arrives on its own. You feel want, then seek sex. This is the model most people assume is normal, because it's how things were early on and how it looks in film.

**Responsive desire** arrives after something starts: touch, closeness, a feeling of being wanted, a context of safety. You don't feel want first. You engage, and want follows.

Research indicates responsive desire is extremely common in long-term relationships and under stress, and particularly common in women, though it occurs across all genders. Under chronic stress, even people who used to have spontaneous desire often shift to responsive.

The problem: many people wait for spontaneous desire, don't feel it, conclude "I don't want to," and decline. Their partner hears rejection. In fact, the desire was available, it just needed a different entry point.

For stressed founder couples this changes the question from "why don't you want me?" to "what would need to happen for want to have a chance?" That's answerable.

| | Spontaneous desire | Responsive desire |
|---|---|---|
| **Sequence** | Want → sex | Closeness/touch → want → sex |
| **Common in** | Early relationships, low stress | Long relationships, high stress |
| **Internal experience** | "I want this" | "I wasn't thinking about it, but now…" |
| **Mistake partners make** | Reads partner's responsive desire as disinterest | Waits for spontaneous desire that won't come, declines |
| **What helps** | Understand partner needs a lead-in | Be willing to start without want, with permission to stop |

## The pattern that outlasts the stress

Here's what makes this dangerous: the biology is temporary. The pattern isn't.

**Partner A** has more desire, or at least more capacity. They initiate. **Partner B**, depleted and distant, declines, or complies without presence, which is worse. A feels rejected and unwanted. They try again, more tentatively, or with an edge. B feels pressured and starts to avoid all physical affection, because a hug might be an opening. Now A is losing not just sex but touch, and B is losing the ability to be close without it meaning something.

After a few months, A stops initiating. B feels relief, and also a quiet loss. Both are lonely. Neither mentions it. The company's crisis passes, cortisol drops, and nothing changes, because the pattern has replaced the problem.

This is the pursue-withdraw cycle in sexual form, and like all patterns it is broken by naming it, not by trying harder inside it.

## Intimacy as another deliverable

Founders and their partners are often high-functioning people who solve things. When sex becomes a problem, the instinct is to manage it: schedule it, optimise it, read about it, set goals.

That instinct is lethal to desire. Desire is one of the few things that gets worse when performance-managed. The moment sex becomes something you're supposed to be doing, achieving, or fixing, it joins the list of obligations, and the body responds to obligation with the same shutdown it responds to stress.

If "we should have sex more" has become an agenda item, the agenda item is the problem.

## Co-founder couples: too close for desire

A specific case. Couples who run a company together have the opposite of the usual problem: not too much distance but too little.

Desire needs some separateness. Some mystery, some sense of the other person as *other*. Co-founder couples share every hour, every decision, every stress. They know each other's inboxes. There is nothing left to discover and nowhere for want to come from.

For these couples the task isn't closeness; it's creating enough distinctness to want to close it. Separate interests. Separate friends. A version of each of you the other doesn't see all day. [Read: Co-founder couples.](/blog/co-founder-couples-married)

## Self-check: What's actually going on?

Separately.

1. When you decline, is it "I don't want to" or "I don't have anything left"?
2. Founder: when you're in bed, where is your attention?
3. Partner: when you're touched, does it feel like pleasure or like a request?
4. Has one of you stopped initiating? Since when?
5. Has one of you started avoiding non-sexual affection?
6. Is there anger or resentment under the surface that hasn't been said?
7. When did you last feel wanted as a person, not needed as a function?
8. If you had a weekend with no company, no children, no obligations, would desire be there? (Be honest. If yes, the problem is capacity and distance. If no, there's something else.)
9. Co-founders: is there anything about your partner you don't already know?

The answers usually point at one of three things: depletion, distance, or pattern. Most couples have at least two.

## What doesn't help

**Scheduling sex.** For some couples, fine. For most stressed couples, it turns desire into a deliverable and kills it.

**"Just make time for the relationship."** Time isn't the constraint. Capacity and connection are.

**Lingerie, hotels, a weekend away.** Changing the setting without changing the state. Two exhausted, distant people in a nice hotel are still exhausted and distant.

**Pressure, or punishing with silence.** Both reinforce the pattern.

**Compliance without presence.** Saying yes with your body and no with your attention teaches your partner that sex with you is lonely.

**Waiting for the company to calm down.** The biology may ease. The pattern won't.

## What helps: Seven practices

### 1. Talk about it, out of the bedroom, without an agenda
A walk. "I miss us, physically and otherwise, and I want to understand what's happened. I'm not asking for anything tonight." The conversation has to be safe before anything else can be.

### 2. Name the pattern as the enemy
"We've got into a thing where you initiate and I can't, and then you stop, and we both end up alone. I don't want that thing." Make it a shared opponent.

### 3. Take sex off the table, deliberately, for a period
And bring back touch that can't lead anywhere. Agree explicitly. The avoider can relax; the pursuer stops being rejected. Paradoxically, this is often when desire returns.

### 4. Founder: come home to your body first
Ten minutes before you walk in. Breath, a walk, anything that moves you out of threat mode. You cannot be present to a partner while your nervous system is still in the board meeting.

### 5. Partner: ask for what makes capacity
Not sex. The thing that would have to happen for sex to be possible: an evening off, a real conversation, being asked how you are. Say it plainly.

### 6. Understand your desire type, and your partner's
If one of you has responsive desire, the ask isn't "want me" but "be willing to start, with full permission to stop." That's a different and achievable request.

### 7. Rebuild connection before chemistry
Every practice in [Founder loneliness, from the partner's side](/blog/founder-loneliness-partner-side) applies here. Desire follows closeness. Fix the distance and the body often follows.

## When to get help

Couples therapy, when the issue is primarily relational: depletion, distance, the pursue-withdraw pattern, unspoken resentment. This is most founder couples.

Sex therapy, when there's a specific sexual difficulty: pain, arousal or orgasm problems, a desire discrepancy that persists after stress and distance are addressed. Many couples therapists have this training; ask.

A doctor, if there could be a physical component: medication side effects (many antidepressants and some blood pressure medications affect desire), hormones, sleep disorders.

## FAQ: Startup stress and intimacy

### Why does sex disappear when you're building a startup?

Chronic stress biologically suppresses desire by keeping the body in threat mode; the relationship often becomes logistical, removing the closeness that desire grows from in long-term couples; and once sex becomes rare, a pursue-and-avoid pattern forms that persists after the stress passes. It's physiology plus distance plus pattern, not a loss of attraction.

### Is it normal for founders to lose interest in sex?

Very. A founder running on adrenaline, little sleep and constant threat-level stress is not biologically available for intimacy. The body deprioritises everything not needed for survival. This is temporary in principle, but the relationship pattern it creates can become permanent if not addressed.

### My partner never wants sex anymore. Is it me?

Almost always no. In founder couples, declining sex is usually about depletion (nothing left) and distance (not feeling close or seen), not attraction. Many people also have responsive rather than spontaneous desire: they don't feel want first, want arrives after closeness starts. Changing the entry point changes the outcome.

### Should we schedule sex to fix it?

For most stressed couples, no. Scheduling turns desire into a deliverable, and desire is one of the few things that gets worse when performance-managed. Scheduling closeness that explicitly can't lead to sex, and letting desire return on its own, works better.

### What is the difference between spontaneous and responsive desire?

Spontaneous desire arrives on its own, before sex. Responsive desire arrives after closeness, touch or feeling wanted begins. Responsive desire is very common in long relationships and under stress. Many people wait for spontaneous desire, don't feel it, and decline, when desire was available through a different door.

### Why do co-founder couples struggle with intimacy differently?

Desire needs some separateness, and co-founder couples have none: shared hours, decisions, stress and inboxes. The problem isn't distance but the absence of distinctness. Separate interests, friends and a version of each other the other doesn't see all day create enough space for want to return.

### Can couples therapy help with intimacy problems caused by startup stress?

Yes. When the cause is relational (depletion, distance, the pursue-avoid pattern, unspoken resentment), couples therapy addresses the source rather than the symptom. For specific sexual difficulties that persist, sex therapy may be added. A good therapist will tell you which you need, and will rule out physical causes with your doctor.
