Search for co-founder coaching and you will find a handful of practitioners, most of them in the Bay Area, Denver or London, almost none of them with a price on the page, and a great deal of language about alignment. Search for co-founder therapy and you will find news stories from 2015 onward about Silicon Valley founders quietly going to couples counsellors, which is roughly how the field started.

Here is what the thing actually is, so you can decide whether you need it.

## Short answer: what is co-founder coaching?

**Co-founder coaching is a series of structured sessions with all the founders of a company present, focused on the working partnership: how you make decisions, how you disagree, who owns what, what you each assumed about the other at the start, and how you repair after a conflict.** The practitioner's client is the partnership, not either founder and not the company.

It is operational rather than personal. A typical session is about a decision that keeps getting reopened, a subject the two of you have avoided for a quarter, or a conversation you need to have — about equity, about performance, about whether one of you still wants to be here — that you want to rehearse before you have it for real.

It is not clinical care, and it is not about anyone's childhood. The methods borrow from couples work, because both deal with two people in a high-stakes relationship with no referee and no easy exit; the content is entirely different.

## Co-founder coaching, co-founder therapy, executive coaching, mediation: the differences

The words get used interchangeably and they should not be.

| Term | Who is in the room | What it works on | When it is right |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Co-founder coaching** | All founders, together | The working partnership: decisions, roles, the unspoken ledger, repair | The company is fine but the partnership is expensive; the same argument keeps recurring |
| **"Co-founder therapy"** | Same as above, in practice | Same as above. The phrase is what founders say when the methods remind them of couples work | A search term more than a service. Clinical therapy is a different thing with a different licence |
| **Executive or founder coaching** | One founder | That person's leadership, communication, decision-making | The issue genuinely sits with one person's development, and both founders agree |
| **Mediation** | Both founders, often with counsel | A defined dispute: equity terms, departure terms | You need an outcome, not an improved relationship |
| **Startup counsel** | One or both founders | Structure: vesting, deadlock provisions, buy-sell, separation | You have agreed what you want and need it to hold |
| **Couples therapy** | Romantic partners | The bond between two people | You are [married to your co-founder](/blog/co-founder-couples-married), and the company is eating the marriage |

The commonest mistake is two founders each getting their own excellent executive coach and wondering why nothing between them changes. The thing between them is not in either room. [We go through what each option fixes and costs in more detail here.](/blog/co-founder-mediation-vs-coaching)

## Who it is for, and when founding teams book it

Founding teams tend to book co-founder coaching at one of four moments.

**Before anything is wrong.** The rarest and the best. A new founding pair, often pre-seed, who have read that [most high-potential startups that fail do so over founder conflict](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide) and would rather write the operating agreement now than discover it under pressure. A few sessions at this stage are worth a year of sessions later.

**When the same argument has come back for the third time.** The company is working. The partnership is expensive. A pricing decision has been open since March, or there is a [50/50 deadlock](/blog/co-founder-5050-deadlock) with no tiebreaker, or one of you has started routing decisions around the other.

**Before something large.** A priced round, an acquisition conversation, a co-founder stepping back to a smaller role, a third co-founder joining. Founding teams who want the partnership solid before the stakes rise.

**When one of you is not sure the partnership survives.** Later than ideal, still often repairable. [A long bad stretch and a finished partnership feel identical from inside](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit), and the room is where you find out which one you have.

It is for founding teams of two or three. It is for teams where the founders are not romantic partners; married co-founders need something that holds the business conversation and the intimate one apart, which is a different piece of work.

## What a session actually covers

Both founders present, online, sixty minutes. An agenda, set in the first few minutes, drawn from whatever is currently stuck. Over a run of sessions, the work usually moves through three things.

**Naming the pattern.** The decisions that get reopened, the resentment that arrives disguised as process, the subject you both steer around. Patterns, not personalities: "we reopen decisions" is a problem two people can attack together, where "you keep second-guessing me" is an accusation that requires a defence.

**Slowing the disagreement down.** Holding a position without it costing the relationship, and conceding without quietly keeping score. This is where the rehearsal happens: the equity conversation or the performance conversation, run once in a room where the stakes are not real, so that the real one goes better. Some founders rehearse between sessions too, against [a model built to hold a position rather than agree](/instruments/conflict).

**Agreeing how you will decide.** You leave with explicit terms for the next hard call: who owns it, what disagreement is allowed to look like, and how the two of you repair afterwards. [The questions that agreement should answer are here](/blog/co-founder-agreement-questions), for teams who want to arrive having thought about them.

Nothing agreed in the room is legally binding. If you decide to add vesting or change decision rights, a lawyer still papers it; the session is where you work out what to ask them for.

## San Francisco and the Bay Area

The Bay Area has more founding teams per square mile than anywhere else, and that density is exactly what makes co-founder conflict harder to work on there.

Your investors know your co-founder's previous investors. Your advisor is on the board of a company your co-founder's best friend runs. The person you would naturally confide in about the partnership is two introductions away from the person you are confiding about. Founders in San Francisco, Palo Alto, Oakland and the Peninsula describe the same thing: there is nobody inside the circle who is neutral, and the board is the last place a half-formed version of a founder conflict should land.

The ecosystem also carries a specific culture about the problem. Y Combinator has been direct for years that founder disputes are the most common thing that kills its companies, and its partners' advice — weekly founder syncs, clear ownership, raise it early — is good and widely ignored under load. Couples counsellors in San Francisco were reporting a doubling in co-founder requests as early as 2015. The demand has been there a long time; the supply of people who do this specifically, rather than as a sideline to executive coaching, is still small.

Co-founder coaching for a Bay Area founding team is therefore usually online with someone outside the local ecosystem, which is both the practical reality and the point: a room with nobody from Sand Hill Road in it.

## New York City

New York founding teams tend to sit across industries rather than inside one — fintech, media, health, climate, consumer — and the pressure arrives differently. Less overlap of advisors, more pressure from fundraising cadence, and a city culture that treats visible disagreement between founders as a signal of weakness to be managed rather than a process to be fixed.

New York also has fewer practitioners who do this specifically. The city is rich in executive coaches for founders and in startup counsel; it is thin on people who will put both co-founders in the same room and work on the thing between them. The result is that NYC founding teams more often go the executive-coaching route — one coach each — and arrive at the same conversation a year later with separately rehearsed positions.

For a New York founding team, the same rule applies as for a Bay Area one. The work is online, both of you present, with someone whose only loyalty is the partnership. Brooklyn and Manhattan on one call is the same session as both of you in one room.

## Why it is almost always online

Three reasons, and none of them is convenience.

**Co-founders are increasingly in different cities.** One in San Francisco, one in New York; one in Austin, one in the Bay Area; one in Oslo, one in Brooklyn. A founding team that only meets in person twice a quarter cannot do this work in person.

**Neutrality.** The practitioner should be outside your ecosystem. For a Bay Area team that usually means outside the Bay Area.

**It works.** The research on online couples work, from which co-founder work borrows its methods, finds outcomes comparable to in-person sessions, and the operational content of co-founder coaching — decisions, roles, the unspoken terms — translates to a video call without loss. Two founders on two screens, cameras on, no interruptions, for an hour, is a better room than most offices offer.

## How to choose a co-founder coach

Ask these, in this order, and pay attention to how quickly the answers come.

**Have you worked with founding teams specifically, and how many?** Co-founder dynamics are not generic business relationships and not couples work. The equity, the irreversibility, the absence of a manager are particular.

**Who is your client — the two of us, or the company?** The answer should be the partnership. Anyone whose client is the company, or who has a relationship with your investors, is not neutral.

**Will both of us be in every session?** Some individual meetings at the start are fine. If the model is mostly alternating one-on-ones, it is executive coaching with a co-founder label.

**What does it cost, in full, and what is the minimum commitment?** If finding out requires a discovery call, ask yourself why.

**When would you tell us this is not repairable?** A practitioner who cannot describe that point is selling sessions.

**Do you have any financial interest in our company or our investors?** An advisor with equity is not neutral, however much you like them.

**Is this coaching or clinical care?** Either is a legitimate answer. Someone who blurs them is not being straight with you.

## How to raise it with your co-founder

This is the part most founders find harder than the sessions.

Frame it as a shared problem, not their problem. "We keep getting stuck in the same place and I'd like help with it" lands very differently from "I think you need to work on this." Say it once, specifically, outside an argument, and give them a few days before expecting an answer.

If they say no, that is information, but it is not necessarily the end. Many co-founders who refuse the first time come to a single session when it is framed as low-stakes and time-boxed: one hour, one subject, no commitment beyond that. A free consultation is useful here precisely because it is nothing to agree to.

If they still will not, individual sessions can help you work out what you want and how to raise it. Be honest with yourself that this is a different and smaller intervention.

## FAQ: co-founder coaching

### What is co-founder coaching?

Structured sessions with all founders present, focused on the working partnership: how you decide, disagree, divide responsibility and repair after conflict. It is operational and forward-looking. It is not executive coaching for one person, not mediation of a single dispute, and not therapy or clinical care, though its methods borrow from couples work.

### Is co-founder coaching the same as co-founder therapy?

In practice, yes: "co-founder therapy" is what founders call it when the methods remind them of couples counselling, and it is the phrase most people search for. Clinical therapy is a separate, licensed activity with a different purpose. Co-founder coaching is work on a business partnership.

### How much does co-founder coaching cost in San Francisco or New York?

Specialist practitioners who publish prices charge roughly $2,000 to $9,000 a month on retainer or around $6,000 for a six-session package, wherever you are, because almost all of it is delivered online. ### Does co-founder coaching work online?

Yes, and for most founding teams it is the only practical format: co-founders are often in different cities, and the practitioner should be outside your local ecosystem. The content — decisions, roles, the conversation you have been avoiding — translates fully to a video call.

### How many sessions does co-founder coaching take?

It depends on what is stuck. A single avoided conversation can move in one or two sessions. A ledger built over three years, or a partnership deciding whether it continues, takes a standing hour for a quarter or more. Practitioners in the field generally describe meaningful change within one to three months when both founders engage in good faith.

### Can we do co-founder coaching if we are not in conflict?

Yes, and it is the best time. A few sessions early in a partnership, to write down decision rights, ambitions and how you will disagree, cost very little and prevent most of what the rest of this cluster is about.
