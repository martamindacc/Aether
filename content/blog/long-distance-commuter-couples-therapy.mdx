You have a good relationship for three days every second weekend, and a strained one for the eleven days in between. Or: you have a fine relationship on the phone and a tense one in person. Or: you have a relationship that works, mostly, except that neither of you can answer the question a friend asked at dinner, "so what's the plan?", and you've stopped asking each other.

Distance relationships are more common than they've ever been. Dual careers, visa timelines, academic posts, deployments, elderly parents in another country, a job in one city and children settled in another. They can work. But they have their own failure modes, and almost nothing written about couples therapy addresses them, because until recently a couple in two places couldn't be in the same therapist's room.

## Short answer: Can therapy help a long-distance relationship?

Yes, and for two-location couples it's often the first time both partners have been fully present for a conversation about the relationship, because the two-screen format puts both of you in the room without either having to travel.

Therapy for distance couples focuses on things one-home couples don't need: the reunion-conflict cycle, the management of visits, maintaining connection through separate daily lives, and, almost always, the unresolved question of when and whether the distance ends. That last question is frequently the real presenting problem, even when the couple arrives talking about something else.

## Chosen distance and imposed distance are different problems

Not all two-location couples are in the same situation, and the distinction matters for what therapy addresses.

**Chosen distance.** Living-apart-together couples, commuter marriages where both partners have careers worth keeping in different cities, couples who prefer separate homes. Here the distance is an arrangement both agreed to, at least initially. The problems tend to be drift, asymmetry (one partner's career is "the reason"), and the slow erosion of a decision that made sense five years ago.

**Imposed distance.** Visa and immigration timelines, deployments, a job that couldn't be refused, a parent who needs care in another country, a child who can't be moved. Here neither partner chose it. The problems tend to be grief, powerlessness, resentment at the circumstance that gets displaced onto the partner, and a timeline that keeps slipping.

**Transitional distance.** The relationship started at a distance, or became distant temporarily with a plan to close it. The problem is usually the plan: it was vague, it keeps moving, or one partner has quietly stopped believing in it.

Therapy with a chosen-distance couple might focus on whether the arrangement still serves them. With an imposed-distance couple, on how to carry something neither of them wanted without turning on each other. With a transitional couple, on the plan.

## The reunion-conflict cycle

The most characteristic pattern of distance relationships, and the one that most often brings couples to therapy.

You've been apart for two weeks. You've been looking forward to the visit. You arrive, or they arrive, and within hours you're arguing about something small. The visit is three days; you've spent one of them repairing. You part on a note of "that didn't go how I wanted," and spend the next two weeks apart carrying that.

Why it happens:

**Accumulated unsaid things.** Small frustrations that would be aired in passing in a one-home relationship get saved up, because you don't want to spend the phone call on them. Then they arrive all at once.

**Different re-entry needs.** One of you wants to collapse into the relationship; the other wants to talk about everything first. (See [when one of you travels](/blog/couples-therapy-when-partner-travels) for the re-entry mechanism in detail; distance couples have it at both ends.)

**Pressure.** The visit has to be good. That pressure makes everything bigger.

**Testing.** Underneath some reunion fights is a question: *do you still want this?* Fighting is one way to find out whether the other person will stay in the room.

The cycle is breakable, but not by trying harder to have a good visit. That's the pressure that creates it.

## The perfect-visit pressure

When time together is scarce, it becomes precious, and precious things get protected into uselessness.

Couples report planning visits like itineraries, avoiding any difficult topic to not "ruin it," performing happiness for each other, and leaving with the sense that they never actually relaxed. Over time, visits become a kind of show both partners put on, and the real relationship happens nowhere.

The fix is counterintuitive: allow visits to be ordinary. Groceries, laundry, a boring evening. The relationship needs ordinary time to be real, and distance couples have to manufacture it deliberately.

## Two daily lives, one relationship

Between visits, each of you lives a complete day the other isn't in. Work, friends, meals, moods, small decisions. In a one-home relationship, these get shared incidentally: a comment over dinner, a face that shows the day went badly. At a distance, they have to be told, and most of them aren't.

So each partner's daily reality becomes gradually opaque to the other. You know their schedule and their big news; you don't know their texture. The relationship becomes a highlights reel.

This isn't a crisis. It's a drift, and it's the one that makes couples arrive at the end of a distance period and discover they don't quite know each other.

## Communication at a distance: too much and too little

Distance couples get communication wrong in two opposite directions.

**Too much.** Constant texting, mandatory nightly calls, location sharing, a sense of surveillance. Communication becomes a duty, the calls become reports, and the relationship feels like a job with a check-in.

**Too little.** Days without real contact, calls that are logistics, a sense that you've become pen pals. Each assumes the other is fine.

What works is usually: fewer, better contacts. One real conversation a day or every couple of days, where you actually talk about how you are, over a hundred texts that say nothing. And an explicit agreement about it, so neither partner is guessing what the other needs.

## Jealousy, trust and the imagination gap

Distance creates an imagination gap. You can't see where they are, who they're with, how they look at someone across a table. Most of the time, this is fine. Some of the time, the imagination fills the gap with fear.

Jealousy in distance relationships is not always about trust in the partner. It's often about the helplessness of the gap itself, and about not having the daily reassurance of presence. It gets worse when communication is thin, when visits have been tense, and when the question of the future is unresolved.

What helps is not more surveillance, which makes everything worse, but more texture: knowing the partner's daily life well enough that the imagination has less space to work in. And addressing the future question, because much distance jealousy is displaced anxiety about whether the relationship is going anywhere.

## The question underneath: when does this end?

Almost every distance couple in therapy is, in the end, talking about this.

If the distance is chosen: is this still what we want, or has it become the thing we don't know how to change?

If imposed: when does it end, and what if the timeline keeps moving? What if one of us stops believing it will?

If transitional: who moves? When? What does the one who moves give up? Has the plan quietly become one partner's plan?

Couples avoid this question because the answer may be difficult, and because in a relationship with scarce time together, nobody wants to spend the visit on it. So it sits underneath every visit and every call, and comes out as reunion conflict, as jealousy, as the sense of drift.

Therapy's first job with most distance couples is to put this question on the table, where it can be worked instead of avoided.

## Deciding to close the distance, or not

When the question is finally asked, there are three honest answers.

**Close it.** Someone moves. This involves loss for the mover (career, place, network) and a shift in power for the couple: the one who moved has given something, and the one who stayed is on home ground. These need to be named and planned for, not discovered after. [See: Relocating for a partner's career](/blog/two-careers-one-relationship), in Hub 3, for the relocation dynamics.

**Keep it, deliberately.** Some couples decide the arrangement serves them and recommit to it with better practices. This is a legitimate outcome, and it's different from drifting along without deciding.

**End it.** Some couples discover that the distance has been what's kept the relationship viable, and that neither wants to close it. That's painful, and it's clearer than years of a plan nobody believes in.

Therapy doesn't make this decision for you. It makes it possible to have the conversation without the visit falling apart.

## Visa-separated and circumstance-separated couples

A specific group with specific weight: couples kept apart by immigration processes, work permits, family care obligations or legal circumstances.

What's different:

- **The powerlessness is real.** You cannot decide to close the distance; a bureaucracy decides.
- **The timeline is external and unreliable.** Dates move. Hope rises and falls on other people's processing times.
- **Resentment has nowhere legitimate to go**, so it goes at the partner, or inward.
- **The relationship becomes a waiting room**, and life is on hold for both.
- **Communication carries extra load:** it's the entire relationship, not a supplement to it.

Therapy here is partly grief work (for the life you're not having), partly practical (how to live rather than wait), and partly protective (how not to turn on each other because of a system neither of you controls). It's also where the two-screen format matters most, since these couples often cannot be in the same place at all.

## Self-check: Where is the distance hurting?

Separately.

1. Describe your last three visits. How much of each was spent in conflict or repair?
2. Do you know what your partner's ordinary Tuesday looks like? Could you describe their day without the highlights?
3. Is communication between visits a pleasure, a duty, or an absence?
4. When did you last talk about when and whether the distance ends? Did you reach anything?
5. Is there a plan? Do you both believe in it?
6. Has one of you quietly built a life in your location that doesn't have room for the other?
7. When you imagine living in one place, what do you feel? (Be honest. Relief? Dread? Nothing?)
8. Is the distance chosen, imposed or transitional, and do you both agree on which?

Question 7 is the one. If the answer surprises you, that's the conversation.

## What doesn't help

**Trying harder to have a perfect visit.** Creates the pressure that wrecks it.

**Saving everything for in person.** The visit gets ambushed; the between-time gets hollow.

**Surveillance as reassurance.** Location sharing and constant check-ins increase anxiety rather than reduce it, and they corrode trust.

**Avoiding the future question to protect the visit.** It's under the visit anyway.

**Treating the distance as the whole problem.** Many distance couples have the same patterns one-home couples have; the distance just hides them.

**Assuming closing the distance will fix things.** It changes things. The patterns come too.

## What helps: Practices for two-location couples

**Let visits be ordinary.** At least one boring evening per visit. Groceries together. The relationship needs mundane time to be real.

**Say small things when they happen.** A two-line message about a frustrating meeting, in the moment, instead of a saved-up list.

**Fewer, better contacts.** Agree the rhythm explicitly. One real conversation a day beats constant texting.

**Share texture, not just news.** What you ate, what you saw, what annoyed you. The ordinary is what the imagination gap needs filling with.

**A re-entry protocol for both ends of every visit.** No information exchange in the first ten minutes. Ten minutes to land. Then talk.

**Schedule the hard conversation, not during the visit.** Have it on a call, with time to think, or in a therapy session. Protect the visit from it by giving it its own time.

**Put the future question on the table, with a date.** When will you decide? What would need to be true? Who's responsible for what information?

**Have something that's only yours together.** A show you only watch together, a book you're both reading, a project. A shared thread across the gap.

## How two-location therapy actually works

This is the format that makes therapy possible for distance couples at all, and it has its own mechanics.

**Two screens, three people.** Each partner joins from their own location. The therapist sees both; each sees the therapist and the other.

**Longer sessions.** 90 minutes as standard. Two-screen work loses the shared physical space, and the extra time compensates.

**More active turn management.** Without the physical cues of a room, the therapist manages who speaks when more explicitly. Couples often find this makes it easier to be heard than in person.

**Each partner in their own safe space.** This is a real advantage for some. Nobody has travelled; nobody is on the other's territory. It can make difficult things easier to say.

**No physical reach.** When a moment calls for one partner to touch the other, they can't. The therapist works with this, and it's one reason an intensive with both of you in the same room is valuable where geography allows.

**Time zone management.** Sessions are scheduled in the overlap. For transatlantic couples, that's typically late morning in the US and late afternoon in Europe. We hold the slot across daylight-saving shifts, which don't align between the US and Europe.

**Continuity across moves.** If one partner relocates mid-therapy, or the distance closes, the therapy continues. The practice isn't a building.

## FAQ: Long-distance and commuter couples therapy

### Can couples therapy work if we live in different cities?

Yes. Two-screen online sessions put both partners in the room with the therapist without either travelling. Sessions run longer (90 minutes) to compensate for the lost shared space, and the therapist manages turns more actively. For many distance couples it's the first time both have been fully present for a conversation about the relationship.

### Why do we always fight when we finally see each other?

The reunion-conflict cycle: accumulated unsaid things arrive at once, re-entry needs differ, the visit carries pressure to be perfect, and sometimes the fight is a test of whether the other will stay. Breaking it requires saying small things as they happen between visits, a re-entry protocol, and letting visits be ordinary rather than perfect.

### How often should long-distance couples talk?

Fewer, better contacts beat constant ones. One real conversation a day or every couple of days, where you actually talk about how you are, outperforms a hundred texts and mandatory nightly calls that become reports. Agree the rhythm explicitly so neither partner is guessing what the other needs.

### Is a commuter marriage sustainable long-term?

It can be, if it's chosen and periodically re-decided rather than drifted into. Risks are asymmetry (one career is "the reason"), erosion of a decision that made sense years ago, and two full lives with a thin overlap. Couples who recommit deliberately, with practices for connection, sustain it; couples who stop asking whether it still serves them often don't.

### How do we decide who moves to close the distance?

By naming what the mover gives up and planning for the power shift (the mover has given something; the stayer is on home ground). Decide together with a date and criteria, not by one partner's plan quietly becoming the default. If one of you feels dread rather than relief at the idea of living together, that's information to work with before anyone moves.

### How does therapy help couples separated by visas or immigration?

Partly grief work for the life you're not living, partly practical help with living rather than waiting, and partly protection against turning on each other because of a system neither controls. Two-screen sessions matter most here, since these couples often can't be in the same place at all during the process.

### What time zones can you work across?

Eastern and Pacific US and Central European as standard, with sessions in the overlap window, typically late morning US / late afternoon Europe for transatlantic couples. The slot holds across the mismatched US and European daylight-saving changes.
