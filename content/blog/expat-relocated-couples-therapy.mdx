Eighteen months in. The boxes are long unpacked, the children have friends, one of you has a job that's going well. From the outside, the move was a success.

Inside the apartment, something has changed that neither of you can name. One of you has a life here. The other has a life that revolves around the first one's life. You speak the local language at different levels. You've made different kinds of friends, or one of you has made friends and the other has made acquaintances. And the question you both avoid, because asking it feels like admitting failure, is sitting in every silence: *are we staying?*

This is not a relationship that moved abroad and ran into problems. This is a relationship running into the specific, predictable problems that moving abroad creates. They have a shape. Knowing it helps.

## Short answer: Why does moving abroad strain a relationship?

Because relocation removes most of the structures that normally hold a relationship up, and then asks the relationship to adapt to a new environment at two different speeds.

The structures: family, old friends, familiar systems, known roles, a shared language with the world around you, a sense of where home is. Gone, or distant, all at once.

The two speeds: one partner usually has a reason for the move (a job, a posting, a return to their own country) and arrives with structure, purpose and often a ready-made social world. The other arrives with none of those and has to build them from scratch, in a place where they may not speak the language, work, or know anyone. The first adapts faster. The gap that opens is the problem.

## The four phases of relocating as a couple

Research on expatriate adjustment describes curves of adaptation for individuals. For couples, the pattern has four recognisable phases, and the relationship is at risk in different ways in each.

| Phase | Typical timing | What's happening | Relationship risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Arrival** | Months 0–4 | Novelty, logistics, us-against-the-world closeness | Low; problems are postponed |
| **2. Dip** | Months 4–12 | Novelty fades, isolation lands, homesickness, friction | Moderate; conflict rises, often about the move itself |
| **3. Divergence** | Year 1–3 | One partner adapts, the other doesn't; separate trajectories | High; the gap becomes the relationship's central fact |
| **4. Stay or go** | Year 2+ | The future question becomes unavoidable | High; couples often disagree, and the disagreement hides in other topics |

Not every couple moves through these in order or on this timing. But most recognise where they are.

## Phase 1: Arrival

The first months are often good for the relationship. There's a shared project: find a home, enrol the children, work out the transport, discover the neighbourhood. You need each other practically. You're both outsiders together. The closeness of the arrival phase is real.

It's also temporary, and it masks things. Problems from before the move are suspended, not resolved. Differences in how each of you is really coping don't show yet, because everyone is coping with the same chaos.

Couples who come to therapy in the arrival phase are rare, and usually arrive with a pre-existing problem the move was supposed to fix. Moving doesn't fix relationships. It pauses them.

## Phase 2: The dip

Somewhere between four months and a year, the novelty fades. The logistics are handled. The partner with the job is absorbed into it. The partner without a structured reason to be there wakes up in a flat in a city where they know nobody, with the children at school and a whole day to fill.

Homesickness lands here, and it lands unevenly. Irritations with the new country surface: the bureaucracy, the weather, the way people are. One partner may defend the country; the other may attack it, and the argument about the country is really an argument about the move.

Conflict rises in the dip. It's often about small things: how the household runs now, money in a new currency, the children's adjustment. Underneath: *you brought us here* and *you agreed to come.*

The dip is survivable and most couples survive it. The danger is what happens next.

## Phase 3: Divergence

This is where expat relationships break.

One partner has a job, colleagues, a reason to leave the house, a developing fluency in the place. They're building a life. The other has been building, too, but from a harder starting point, and often more slowly. Or they've stopped building and started waiting.

By year two or three, the two partners are often living in different countries emotionally. One belongs here. The other is a resident alien in their own relationship.

Divergence shows up as:
- One partner's social life being the couple's social life
- Different language levels, and the gap between them growing
- One partner wanting to explore and settle; the other wanting to go home
- The adapted partner feeling responsible for the other's unhappiness and becoming defensive
- The unadapted partner feeling like a dependent, and hating it
- Arguments about everything except the thing

The divergence phase is when couples most often seek therapy, and it's when therapy can do the most, because the gap is still a gap and not yet a decision.

## Phase 4: Stay, go, or go somewhere else

Eventually the question becomes unavoidable: a contract ends, a child reaches a school transition, a parent gets ill, a visa needs renewing, an offer comes from somewhere else.

Couples in divergence often disagree about the answer, and the disagreement is painful because it's not symmetrical. The adapted partner is asking the other to keep sacrificing. The unadapted partner is asking the other to give up something they've built. Neither is being unreasonable.

What makes this phase dangerous is avoidance. Because the question is hard, couples don't ask it, and it leaks into everything else: the arguments about the children's schooling, the resentment about the holidays, the silence after a dinner party where someone asked "so what's the plan?"

The couples who come through phase four together are the ones who put the question on the table before circumstance forces it, with a date and criteria for deciding.

## The trailing partner's identity collapse

The partner who moved for the other's reason has a name in the relocation literature, trailing spouse, and a well-documented experience that deserves its own section.

Before the move, they had a career, a social role, a competence in their own world. After it, often: no work permit, or no recognised qualifications, or no job market in their field. No friends of their own. A language they don't speak. A role that has collapsed to supporting the partner and running the home, which may or may not be a role they ever wanted.

Identity collapse is not too strong a term. Many trailing partners describe not knowing who they are in the new place, and being ashamed of struggling when the move was "an adventure" or "a great opportunity."

The relationship consequences: dependency on the partner that neither wanted; resentment that has nowhere legitimate to go; a loss of the equal footing the relationship was built on; and, sometimes, a quiet decision by the trailing partner that they will not do this again, which surfaces as a hard no when the next move comes.

If you're the trailing partner, this experience is documented, common, and not a sign that you're failing at expatriation. If you're the partner with the job, your partner's struggle is not an accusation, and it will not resolve by them "putting themselves out there more."

## "We only have each other": the overload problem

Far from family and old friends, expat couples often become each other's entire support system. Partner, best friend, therapist, co-parent, only confidant.

It sounds romantic. It is structurally unsustainable. One relationship cannot carry the load that a whole network used to carry. Small frictions become big because there's no one else to vent to. Each partner's bad day lands on the other with nothing to buffer it. And the pressure to be everything for each other makes both feel they're failing.

Expat couples need outside connection not as a threat to the relationship but as a relief for it. The couple is healthier when each partner has a life the other isn't the centre of.

## Serial relocators and the family that never lands

Some couples move repeatedly: diplomatic postings, corporate rotations, academic careers, military. The phases compress and repeat, and some things never get to phase one.

Serial relocation adds: the exhaustion of rebuilding again, the gradual refusal of one partner (usually the trailing one) to invest in each new place because it won't last, children who stop making friends, and a relationship that has no fixed point anywhere, not even a home country.

Therapy for serial relocators often focuses on what the couple carries between places, because that portable thing, whatever it is, is the home.

## Repatriation: the move nobody prepares for

Going home is often harder than leaving was, and almost no one expects it.

The couple returns changed. Home didn't change, or changed without them. Friends have moved on. The things that were difficult abroad become the things they miss. The partner who adapted abroad may now be the one struggling. The children may be the most foreign people in the room.

Repatriation is a phase one to four cycle in reverse, compressed, without the excuse of a foreign country to explain the difficulty. Couples who have survived the expatriation often split in the repatriation, because they thought the hard part was over and stopped paying attention.

## Self-check: Which phase are you in?

Separately.

1. Who had the reason for the move? Who came along?
2. Describe your social life here. Then your partner's. Are they the same list?
3. Language: where is each of you? Is the gap growing?
4. When you talk about the host country, does one of you defend it and the other criticise it?
5. Has one of you quietly stopped investing in this place?
6. When did you last talk, directly, about whether you're staying? Did you reach anything?
7. Is your partner your only confidant here? Are you theirs?
8. If you had to decide in six months, would you agree?

If your answers to 2, 5 and 8 diverge, you're in phase three heading toward four. That's the right time for help.

## What doesn't help

**"Put yourself out there more."** Said by the adapted partner to the unadapted one. It locates the problem in the struggling partner's effort rather than in the structure.

**Defending the country.** The argument is never about the country.

**Deferring the stay-or-go question to protect the present.** It's in the present anyway.

**Expecting the move to resolve pre-existing problems.** It paused them. They're waiting.

**The couple as the only support.** Overload, not intimacy.

**Assuming repatriation will be easy.** It's often the hardest phase.

## What helps: Practices for relocated couples

**Name the asymmetry explicitly.** "I had the reason; you came along. I know that's not the same." Said by the partner with the job, early and more than once.

**Build the trailing partner's life first.** Not the home, not the children's activities. Their work, their friends, their language, their reason to leave the house. Make it a shared project with the same priority as the job that brought you.

**Get support outside the couple.** Friends, a therapist, a community of other internationals, a club. For both of you. Not as escape; as relief.

**Have the stay-or-go conversation on a schedule.** Every six months, formally. What would need to be true to stay? To go? Who's finding out what?

**Separate the country from the move from the relationship.** Three different things. When you argue, which one is it?

**Hold one thing that's yours and unchanging.** A ritual, a meal, a way of being together that isn't about the place. For serial relocators, this is the home.

**Prepare for repatriation like a move abroad.** Because it is one.

## How therapy works for expat couples

**A therapist who isn't local, on purpose.** In small expat communities, the local English-speaking therapist knows everyone. Online therapy from a practice outside your city gives you a clinician who doesn't attend the same school events.

**Fluency in the relocation arc.** You shouldn't have to explain what a trailing spouse is, or why year two is harder than year one. [For the Norwegian system specifically, see our Oslo guide.](/blog/couples-therapy-oslo-english)

**Cultural frames held, not imposed.** A therapist from the host culture may treat its norms as the norm. A therapist from one partner's home culture may side with that partner's frame. Therapy for international couples needs a clinician who can hold both as valid. [More in our cross-cultural couples article.](/blog/bilingual-cross-cultural-couples-therapy)

**Continuity through moves.** If you relocate again, or repatriate, the therapy comes with you. For serial relocators, this may be the only continuous professional relationship in their lives.

**The stay-or-go question, handled directly.** Expat couples therapy almost always has to go here, and a therapist experienced with relocated couples will go there deliberately rather than letting it leak.

## FAQ: Expat and relocated couples therapy

### Why do relationships struggle after moving abroad?

Because relocation removes the structures that held the relationship up (family, friends, familiar systems, shared language with the world) and then asks both partners to adapt at different speeds. The one with the reason for the move adapts faster; the trailing partner starts from scratch. The gap that opens, usually in years one to three, becomes the relationship's central strain.

### What are the phases of expat relationship adjustment?

Arrival (months 0–4: novelty, closeness, problems postponed), the dip (months 4–12: isolation, homesickness, conflict about the move), divergence (years 1–3: one partner adapts, the other doesn't), and stay-or-go (year 2+: the future question becomes unavoidable). Relationships most often break in divergence, and therapy helps most there.

### What is trailing spouse syndrome?

The well-documented experience of the partner who moved for the other's reason: loss of career, social role, independence and often work rights, leading to identity collapse, dependency neither partner wanted, and resentment with nowhere legitimate to go. It is common, structural, and not a sign the trailing partner is failing at expatriation.

### How do expat couples decide whether to stay or go home?

By asking the question on a schedule, every six months, with criteria for each answer, before circumstance forces it. The disagreement is usually asymmetrical (one partner is asking the other to keep sacrificing; the other is asking them to give up what they've built), and naming that asymmetry is the start. Avoiding the question doesn't protect the present; it leaks into it.

### Is repatriation harder than moving abroad?

Often, yes. The couple returns changed to a home that didn't change with them, without the excuse of a foreign country to explain the difficulty, and having assumed the hard part was over. Couples who survived expatriation sometimes split in repatriation because they stopped paying attention. Prepare for it like a move abroad.

### Should expat couples see a local therapist or one online from elsewhere?

For many, online from outside the city is better: a clinician who isn't part of the same small expat community, who understands the relocation arc, who can hold both partners' cultural frames, and who continues with you if you move again. A local therapist can work when the community is large enough for privacy and the therapist has international experience.

### Can couples therapy help if we only have each other here?

Yes, and part of the work is reducing that overload. One relationship can't carry what a whole network used to. Therapy helps couples build outside support for each partner, not as a threat to the relationship but as relief for it, while also giving the couple a third person in the room who isn't either of them.
