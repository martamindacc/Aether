By the time a founding team goes looking for outside help, there is usually a specific thing they want solved and a general thing they are afraid of. The specific thing sends them to the wrong specialist, and the general thing is what actually needed addressing.

Here is what each option can and cannot change.

## Short answer: which one do you need?

A rough rule that holds most of the time:

- **A defined dispute to settle, and the working relationship is secondary** — mediation.
- **The working relationship is the problem, and the company is otherwise fine** — co-founder coaching.
- **You have agreed what you want and need it to hold** — a startup lawyer.
- **One founder needs to develop as a leader** — executive coaching, individually.

The commonest error is reaching for the legal layer when what is actually broken is how the two of you decide. Lawyers produce excellent documents and cannot make two people work together.

## The four options, and what each one changes

### Mediation

**What it is.** A neutral third party runs a structured process toward a specific settlement. They do not take sides and do not impose an outcome; they manage the conversation until there is an agreement both parties will sign.

**What it changes.** The terms of a particular dispute — an equity disagreement, the conditions of a departure, who owns what.

**What it does not change.** How the two of you operate afterwards. Mediation is built to conclude something, not to improve a partnership. If you mediate an equity dispute and then continue running the company together, the mechanism that produced the dispute is still in place.

**When it is right.** There is a concrete thing to settle, positions are entrenched, and you need an outcome rather than an understanding. Especially useful during a separation, where it is dramatically cheaper and faster than two sets of counsel.

**Cost and shape.** Typically a small number of long sessions, often one or two full days.

### Co-founder coaching

**What it is.** Both founders, repeatedly, with someone whose focus is the working partnership itself — how you decide, disagree, divide responsibility and repair after conflict.

**What it changes.** The patterns. Decision rights, the unspoken ledger, the subject you have been avoiding, the mechanism you never installed. It is forward-looking: the aim is a partnership that works better next quarter, not a settlement of last quarter.

**What it does not change.** Legal structure. Nothing agreed in a coaching room is binding; if you decide to change vesting, a lawyer still papers it.

**When it is right.** The company is functioning but the partnership is expensive. You keep having the same argument. You cannot tell any more which of your problems are strategic and which are relational. You are approaching something large — a raise, an acquisition offer, a co-founder stepping back — and want the partnership solid before you get there.

**Cost and shape.** Recurring sessions over weeks or months, both founders present. Some practitioners open with one individual meeting per founder before the joint work; the joint sessions are where the change happens.

### A startup lawyer

**What it is.** Counsel on structure: vesting, deadlock provisions, buy-sell mechanics, shareholders' agreements, separation terms.

**What it changes.** What is enforceable. The fire exits, the tiebreakers, who can do what to whom.

**What it does not change.** Whether you should. Counsel will tell you what is possible and what it exposes you to. They cannot tell you whether this partnership is worth keeping, and asking them to is asking the wrong question of the right person.

**When it is right.** You know what you want and need it to hold. Also: always, eventually. Every structural fix in this cluster ends with a lawyer papering it.

**The trap.** Bringing counsel in before you have agreed the shape converts a conversation between partners into a negotiation between parties. That transition is almost impossible to reverse, and it is the single most common way a repairable partnership becomes an unrepairable one.

### Executive coaching

**What it is.** One founder, one coach, working on that founder's development as a leader.

**What it changes.** That person — how they communicate, delegate, handle pressure, make decisions.

**What it does not change.** The relationship. Two founders each with their own excellent coach can still be entirely stuck, because the thing between them is not in either room. It can even make things worse: two founders arriving at the same conversation with separately-rehearsed positions and separately-validated grievances is a harder conversation than the one they were having before.

**When it is right.** The issue genuinely sits with one person's capability, and both of you agree on that. Aether offers it as [executive and founder work](/executive-founder-work), alongside co-founder sessions for the two of you.

## A decision table

| Your situation | What you need |
|---|---|
| We cannot agree on equity and it is entrenched | Mediation, then counsel |
| We keep having the same argument every month | Co-founder coaching |
| One of us is leaving and we need terms | Mediation, then counsel |
| We decided to add vesting | Counsel |
| Decisions get made and then reopened | Co-founder coaching |
| We are [deadlocked and have no tiebreaker](/blog/co-founder-5050-deadlock) | Coaching for the mechanism, counsel to paper it |
| There is a subject we have avoided for a year | Co-founder coaching |
| [One founder has stopped pulling their weight](/blog/co-founder-not-pulling-weight) | Co-founder coaching first; counsel only if an exit follows |
| One founder needs to grow into the CEO role | Executive coaching |
| We do not know if this partnership survives | Co-founder coaching |
| Trust is broken by a specific incident | Coaching if either of you wants to repair it; mediation if neither does |
| We are married and run the company together | [Couples therapy that understands the company](/blog/co-founder-couples-married), with coaching for the business layer |

## What each option costs

Prices are rarely published in this field, which is itself a problem for founders trying to decide. In rough order of what a founding team actually spends: a contested separation through two sets of counsel runs into tens of thousands of dollars; a mediator charges a day rate, usually a few thousand dollars for one or two long days; specialist co-founder coaching retainers run from about $2,000 to $9,000 a month; executive coaching for one founder runs $200 to $600 an hour; and an Aether Practice co-founder session is ${{price:cofounder-session:usd}} for sixty minutes with both founders present. Two things are worth noticing in that order. The expensive option is the one founders reach for first. And the thing that most often decides whether the partnership survives — a structured conversation with both of you in the room — is the cheapest item on it.

## Why founders call the lawyer first

Three reasons, all understandable.

**It feels like control.** Everything else in the situation is ambiguous. Legal advice produces a concrete document, and in a crisis that is enormously appealing.

**It is a legitimate expense.** A legal bill is a line item nobody questions. Coaching for the founding relationship sounds, to a founder worried about how things look, like admitting a weakness — even though it is cheaper by an order of magnitude and addresses the thing actually breaking.

**It does not require admitting anything to the other founder.** You can call a lawyer alone. Everything else on this list requires saying to your co-founder that you think there is a problem, which is the hard part and the part that actually helps.

That last one is worth sitting with. If you have engaged counsel about your co-founder without telling them, you have already made a decision about the partnership. Worth knowing that about yourself before the process makes it for you.

## What co-founder coaching looks like in practice

Since it is the least familiar of the four, specifically:

**Both of you are in the room.** Not alternating individual sessions. The work is the thing between you, and it cannot be done in two separate conversations.

**It is structured, not open-ended.** The sessions have an agenda — the pattern you are stuck in, the decision rights you never defined, the conversation you have been avoiding. It is not a venting session, and a good practitioner will stop it becoming one.

**The content is operational, not personal.** How you decide. Who owns what. What you each thought you agreed in year one. It is about the working partnership; it is not clinical care, and it is not about anyone's childhood.

**The practitioner is loyal to the partnership, not to either of you.** This is the thing none of your advisors, investors or friends can offer, and it is most of the value. Not a tiebreaker, not an adjudicator — someone with no interest in which of you is right.

**Rehearsal is part of it.** The hardest conversations — equity, performance, whether one of you still wants to be here — go better when they have been practised once in a room where the stakes are not real. Founders who rehearse hold their position more clearly and concede less for the sake of ending the discomfort. **It is usually online.** Co-founders in different cities are now ordinary, and the two of you in two offices on one call is the same session as the two of you on one sofa. ## Questions to ask before you hire anyone

**Have you worked with founding teams specifically?** Co-founder dynamics are not generic business relationships and not couples work. The equity, the irreversibility, the absence of a manager — these are particular.

**Who is your client — us, or the company?** The answer should be the partnership. Anyone whose answer is the company, or who has a relationship with your investors, is not neutral.

**What happens to what we say?** Confidentiality between the two founders, and from the board, should be explicit.

**What does success look like, and when would you tell us this is not working?** A practitioner who cannot describe the point at which they would say "this is not repairable, here is what to do instead" is selling sessions.

**Do you have a financial interest in our company?** An advisor with equity is not neutral, however much you like them.

**What does it cost, in full?** If the answer requires a discovery call to find out, ask yourself why.

## What about your investors, your board, your advisors?

Reasonable to consider; usually not the answer.

**Investors are structurally non-neutral.** Their obligation runs to the asset. A board member who likes you both will still, correctly, optimise for the company — and if one founder is the problem for the company, that is what the advice will reflect. Founders know this, which is why they bring the board a managed version of the conflict, which means the advice is based on partial information anyway.

**Advisors and friends take a side** — usually whoever told them first. Once each founder has sympathisers, the conflict has a constituency and becomes much harder to resolve privately.

**Other founders are useful for normalising and useless for resolving.** Hearing that someone else went through it helps. Their specific advice is about their partnership, not yours.

Keep the conflict out of the ecosystem until you have a resolution. Then tell the board the resolution.

## FAQ: getting help with co-founder conflict

### What is the difference between co-founder coaching and co-founder mediation?

Mediation settles a defined dispute — an equity disagreement, the terms of a departure — and ends when there is an agreement to sign. Co-founder coaching works on the partnership itself over several sessions: how you decide, disagree and divide responsibility, so the mechanism that produced the dispute changes. Use mediation when you need an outcome. Use coaching when you need the working relationship to work.

### Is co-founder coaching the same as couples therapy or co-founder therapy?

Some of the method overlaps, because both deal with two people in a high-stakes relationship with no referee and no easy exit, and many practitioners borrow from Gottman and emotionally focused work. The content is entirely different: decision rights, contribution and direction rather than intimacy. It is coaching for a working partnership, not clinical care. People search for "co-founder therapy" and mean this.

### How much does co-founder mediation cost compared with coaching?

Mediation is usually priced by the day, typically a few thousand dollars for one or two long sessions, and is far cheaper than a separation negotiated through two sets of lawyers. ### Will our investors find out?

Not from the practitioner. Whether you tell them is your call. Many founding teams do, after the fact, and most investors regard a team that invested in its own partnership as a lower risk rather than a higher one.

### What if my co-founder will not come?

Ask once, specifically, framed as a shared problem rather than their problem — "we keep getting stuck in the same place and I'd like help with it" lands very differently from "I think you need to work on this." If they still will not, individual sessions can help you work out what you want and how to raise it, but be honest that it is a different and smaller intervention.

### How long does co-founder coaching take to work?

Depends what is wrong. A single avoided conversation can move in one or two sessions. A ledger built over three years, or a partnership deciding whether it continues, takes longer — practitioners in the field generally describe meaningful change within one to three months of regular sessions when both founders engage in good faith.

### Is it worth it if we are probably going to separate anyway?

Often, yes — for two reasons. You may find you are not. And if you are, founding teams that have done this work separate far better, which protects the company and both of your reputations. [Separation is its own skill.](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit)

### Can we do co-founder coaching online?

Yes, and for founding teams in different cities or time zones it is usually the only practical format. Aether Practice works entirely online with founding teams in San Francisco, New York City and Norway.
