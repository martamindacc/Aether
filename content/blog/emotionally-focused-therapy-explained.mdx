Emotionally Focused Therapy is a couples therapy approach built on attachment science. It treats relationship distress as a negative cycle in which each partner's protective moves (criticising, withdrawing, defending) trigger the other's, and it works in three stages: de-escalating the cycle, restructuring how partners reach for each other, and consolidating the new pattern. EFT has the strongest clinical-trial evidence of any couples method, including a 2024 meta-analysis and two-year follow-up data, and roughly 70–75% of couples in early trials moved from distressed to non-distressed. It is the primary method at Aether Practice. This article walks through the three stages using one composite couple, explains the "softening" event that defines the middle stage, gives an honest reading of the evidence, and says who EFT fits and who it doesn't.

## The idea underneath it

EFT starts from a claim that sounds soft and turns out to be rigorous: adults need secure emotional connection the way children do, and most of what looks like a communication problem in a couple is actually a connection problem.

When a partner feels that connection is threatened, they protest. The protest takes one of two forms. Some people move toward: they raise their voice, criticise, press for a response. Some people move away: they go quiet, get busy, minimise. Both are attempts to manage the same fear. Both tend to produce exactly the response that confirms the fear.

That loop is what EFT calls the negative cycle. The therapist's first job is to make it visible. The second is to get underneath it. The third is to help the couple build something else.

## One couple, three stages

To make the stages concrete, here is a composite couple drawn from our practice. Details are changed and no individual is described.

Maya and Daniel, both in their late thirties, both in demanding roles. They came in because "we argue about everything and nothing." Daniel's account: Maya is critical and nothing he does is enough. Maya's account: Daniel is never there, and when she tries to talk about it he shuts down. Neither of them is wrong.

### Stage 1: De-escalation (sessions 1–5 or so)

The therapist's work in Stage 1 is to track the cycle in the room, slow it down, and name it in a way both partners recognise.

By the second session the sequence was clear. Maya raises something, usually with an edge. Daniel hears attack, goes still, answers in short sentences or changes the subject. Maya experiences the stillness as not caring and presses harder. Daniel experiences the pressing as proof he's failing and withdraws further. By the time either of them notices, Maya is furious and Daniel is somewhere else.

The therapist laid this out, slowly, checking each step with each of them. Then the reframe that defines Stage 1: the cycle is the problem, not either of you. Maya isn't a critic; she's someone who's frightened of not mattering and shows it as anger. Daniel isn't cold; he's someone who's frightened of never getting it right and shows it as distance.

What changed: they started catching it. "We're doing the thing," Maya said one evening, mid-escalation, and Daniel laughed, and the argument lost its grip. That's the Stage 1 outcome. Not resolution. De-escalation. The cycle still runs, but it's a thing they can see.

> **From our practice.** Stage 1 is where most couples first feel relief, and it's also where some stop, because the fighting has eased and it feels like enough. It usually isn't. A cycle that's been de-escalated but not restructured comes back under pressure. We say this explicitly around session four.

### Stage 2: Restructuring (sessions 6–14 or so)

Stage 2 is where EFT differs most from skills-based approaches, and where the actual change happens.

The therapist works with one partner at a time, slowly, while the other listens. The questions get more specific and quieter. Not "what do you do when she criticises you" but "when she says that, what's the first thing you feel, before the shutting down?"

For Daniel, the answer took three sessions to reach. Underneath the stillness wasn't indifference. It was shame: a conviction that he was going to fail her no matter what he did, and that going quiet was the only way to not make it worse. He'd never said that to anyone. He hadn't quite known it himself.

The therapist then helped him say it to Maya. Not to the therapist, about Maya. To Maya, looking at her: "When you come at me, I don't go cold. I go to a place where I'm sure I'm failing you and there's nothing I can do. So I disappear. I don't want to."

This is what EFT calls withdrawer re-engagement. The partner who has been absent comes back into the room with something real.

Maya's turn came two sessions later, and it was harder, because the pursuing partner is usually the one who has been "strong" and has the most to lose by being seen as frightened. Underneath the criticism was a question she had been asking for years in the only language that seemed to get a response: do I matter to you at all? The therapist helped her ask it directly, without the edge. "I push because I'm scared you've stopped seeing me. I need to know I'm still someone you want."

This is the softening event: the moment the pursuing partner drops the protest and asks for what they need, and the other partner, now re-engaged, is able to respond. In EFT research, the softening is the single change event most strongly associated with lasting improvement. It isn't dramatic from outside. The room goes quiet. Someone usually cries. From inside, couples describe it as the first time in years they've actually reached each other.

### Stage 3: Consolidation (final 2–4 sessions)

Stage 3 is shorter and more practical. The couple has a new way of reaching each other; the work is to make it the default.

Maya and Daniel revisited an old perpetual argument (money, in their case) and discovered it went differently when both of them were in the room. They built two small rituals: ten minutes after the children were down, and an explicit signal ("I'm going to the shame place") that Daniel could use when he felt himself leaving. They talked about what would pull them back into the old cycle under stress, and what they'd do.

They finished at session fourteen. The cycle didn't vanish; they said they still fell into it about once a month. The difference was that it now lasted twenty minutes instead of three days, and one of them could always find the way out.

## What the therapist is actually doing

EFT is sometimes described as "just talking about feelings," which misses how technical it is. In the room the therapist is doing specific things:

- **Tracking the cycle** in real time and reflecting it back, move by move.
- **Slowing down** past the first emotion to the one underneath. Anger is almost always the top layer; fear, shame or grief is usually what's protected.
- **Heightening** the moments that matter, so a partner's real need lands instead of sliding past.
- **Choreographing enactments:** having one partner say the thing directly to the other, and helping the other receive it, rather than letting it be said about them to the therapist.
- **Reframing** protective behaviour as attachment protest, so partners stop reading each other's worst moments as character.

Homework is minimal. The change is designed to happen in session, where the therapist can hold both partners through the hard moment, and then to be practised at home because it has already been experienced, not because it was assigned.

## What the research says

EFT has the strongest clinical-trial evidence of any couples approach. The honest picture:

**Outcomes.** A 2024 meta-analysis of EFT trials (Spengler and colleagues) found large effects on relationship satisfaction and attachment measures, consistent across studies. The earlier 1999 meta-analysis by Johnson and colleagues reported that roughly 70–75% of couples in trials moved from distressed to the non-distressed range, with around 90% showing significant improvement; those figures have been widely repeated and the 2024 synthesis broadly supports the direction, if not always the exact numbers.

**Durability.** A two-year follow-up study (Wiebe and colleagues, 2017) found gains in satisfaction and attachment security were maintained, and in some couples continued to improve after therapy ended. Continued improvement is unusual in couples research and is attributed to the couple having changed how they relate rather than having learned a skill they might stop using.

**Mechanism.** Process research has identified the softening event as the change moment most strongly linked to outcome, and has shown that couples who complete a softening have better follow-up results than those who don't. This matters because it means EFT's theory of change has been tested, not only its results.

**Limits.** The trials are mostly North American and mostly with moderately distressed couples. Evidence for EFT with severe distress, with couples where one partner has an untreated condition, or across cultures is thinner. EFT also requires emotional access; couples where one partner cannot or will not go beneath the surface progress slowly.

**Compared with other methods.** The 2020 Roddy meta-analysis of couple therapy overall found that established methods produce comparable large effects, with therapist and alliance factors mattering more than model. EFT's advantage is the depth and consistency of its own trial base, not a demonstrated superiority over every alternative in head-to-head comparison.

We use EFT because its theory of change matches what we see in the room, because the evidence for durability is the best available, and because the couples we most often work with (distant, high-functioning, "we don't fight, we just don't connect") are exactly the ones the method was built for.

## Who EFT fits

- **Distance and withdrawal.** The quiet couple who coordinate well and have stopped reaching. This is EFT's home ground.
- **Pursue–withdraw cycles.** One raises, one shuts down. The most common presentation in couples therapy and the one EFT maps most precisely.
- **Attachment injuries.** A betrayal, an abandonment at a critical moment, a failure to show up when it mattered. EFT has a specific, researched model for repairing these.
- **Couples who have done skills-based work and found it didn't hold.** If you've learned the techniques and the cycle keeps coming back, the layer underneath hasn't been touched.
- **High-functioning, composed people.** EFT's emphasis on duration and slowing down is one of the few things that reliably works past a polished surface, which is why it suits [executive and founder couples](/executive-founder-work) and why we pair it with intensive formats.

## Who it fits less well

- **Couples who want tools first.** If you'd rather have a framework, a checklist and homework, a structured approach may feel more productive. EFT can feel slow or abstract in the first sessions.
- **Couples where one partner won't go beneath the surface.** EFT needs emotional access. A partner who will discuss behaviour but not feeling slows the work considerably.
- **Relationships with control, intimidation or violence.** EFT asks partners to be vulnerable; that's unsafe where one partner is afraid. Individual support first.
- **An ongoing or undisclosed affair.** No couples method works with an active secret. Individual work first.
- **Severe untreated individual conditions.** Active addiction, acute depression, untreated trauma. These usually need their own treatment alongside or before couples work.

In those cases we'll say so in the consultation and point you to what fits.

## EFT and the Gottman Method, briefly

Couples often arrive having read about both. They're compatible in practice and most therapists draw on both, but the primary lens differs: the Gottman approach asks "what behaviours need to change" and EFT asks "what is this cycle protecting." Gottman-based work is structured and homework-led and suits conflict-heavy couples who want tools; EFT is process-led and suits distant or withdrawn couples. The clinical-trial evidence is stronger for EFT; the observational research underlying Gottman's framework has shaped the whole field, ours included. ## What EFT looks like at Aether Practice

We follow the three-stage structure above, with some adaptations for the couples we work with most.

**We move into the cycle quickly.** By the end of the first or second session we'll have named a hypothesis about your pattern and checked it with both of you. Couples with limited time disengage from process that doesn't produce; we don't spend six sessions building rapport before touching anything.

**We see each partner alone early.** Twenty to thirty minutes each, in the first or second session. This is standard EFT practice and it's also how we catch the things that would make couples work unsafe or premature.

**We use intensive formats where they fit.** Stage 2 work in particular benefits from duration: a two-day intensive often reaches a softening that would take weeks of 50-minute sessions, because neither partner can leave at the hard moment. What happens in a two-day intensive describes the arc.

**We work fully online, with couples across the US and in Norway.** EFT translates well to video: both faces are visible at once, which makes the moment-by-moment tracking easier than in a room.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Emotionally Focused Therapy?

A couples therapy approach grounded in attachment science. It treats relationship distress as a negative cycle in which each partner's protective moves trigger the other's, and works in three stages: de-escalating the cycle, restructuring how partners reach each other, and consolidating the new pattern. It was developed by Sue Johnson and Les Greenberg in the 1980s and has the strongest clinical-trial evidence of any couples method.

### How many sessions does EFT take?

Typically 8–20 sessions. Stage 1 usually takes four to six, Stage 2 is the longest at six to ten, and Stage 3 is two to four. Intensive formats compress Stages 1 and 2 into two consecutive days with follow-ups.

### What is the "negative cycle" in EFT?

The repeating sequence of protective moves a couple falls into regardless of topic: most often one partner pursuing (criticising, pressing) and the other withdrawing (going quiet, leaving), each move triggering the other's. EFT's first stage is making this cycle visible so the couple can see it as a shared problem rather than as something one partner does to the other.

### What is a softening event?

The moment in Stage 2 when the pursuing partner drops the protest and asks directly for what they need, and the re-engaged withdrawing partner is able to respond. In EFT research it's the change event most strongly linked to lasting improvement. From outside it's quiet; from inside, couples describe it as the first time in years they've reached each other.

### Does EFT work?

Yes, with the best evidence of any couples method. A 2024 meta-analysis found large, consistent effects on satisfaction and attachment; early trials reported roughly 70–75% of couples moving from distressed to non-distressed; and two-year follow-up shows gains hold and sometimes continue to grow after therapy ends.

### Is EFT better than the Gottman Method?

It has stronger clinical-trial evidence, but the two serve somewhat different couples. EFT suits distance, withdrawal and attachment injuries; Gottman-based work suits conflict-heavy couples who want structured tools. Most experienced therapists draw on both. Choose by your problem and your therapist, not by brand.

### Does EFT involve homework?

Very little. Change is designed to happen in session, where the therapist can hold both partners through difficult moments, and then to be practised at home because it's been experienced rather than assigned. Couples who want structured exercises between sessions may prefer a different approach.

### Can EFT be done online?

Yes. Video-delivered EFT has been compared directly with in-person EFT with comparable outcomes on satisfaction and attachment measures. The method's moment-by-moment tracking of both partners translates well to a format where both faces are visible at once.

### Can EFT help after an affair?

Yes. EFT has a specific model for repairing attachment injuries, including infidelity, and research shows couples who complete the injury-resolution process have better outcomes than those who don't. It's used alongside stabilisation and disclosure work; see [couples therapy after infidelity](/blog/couples-therapy-after-infidelity).
