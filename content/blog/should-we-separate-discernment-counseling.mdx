The thought has been there a long time. It comes in the evening, in the car, in meetings. *Should we really keep going?*

You haven't said it out loud. Maybe you've hinted at it, and maybe your partner went on the defensive, or into panic. Maybe your partner is the one who said it, and you're the one who can't take it in.

You're in a kind of no man's land. Not together enough to be okay. Not finished enough to leave. And every day that passes, it gets a little heavier to stay there.

This is the situation discernment counseling was built for.

## Short answer: How do I know if we should separate?

You rarely know by thinking more. You know by examining three things honestly:

1. **What has actually happened to us?** Not the surface, but the pattern underneath.
2. **What is my own part in it?** Not to take the blame, but because you can't assess whether it can change without knowing.
3. **What would have to change for me to stay, and is that realistic?**

Most people who consider separating have never done these three things together with their partner, in a safe room. When they do, the answer often becomes clear, in one direction or the other.

## Why "should we separate?" is the wrong question to start with

Because it's a yes/no question asked of a situation that isn't yes/no.

When you ask "should we separate?", your brain looks for evidence. And it finds it: all the fights, all the distance, all the times you were let down. You build a case. Your partner builds a counter-case. You're in a trial, not a conversation.

The question that actually produces answers is: **"What has happened to us, and can it change?"** That's a question you can examine together. The first one you can only fight about.

## What is discernment counseling?

Discernment counseling is a short, bounded form of couples work, typically 1–5 sessions, for couples where at least one partner is unsure whether the relationship should continue.

It differs from regular couples therapy on one essential point: **The goal is not to improve the relationship. The goal is to make a decision about the relationship.**

In discernment counseling:

- You meet the therapist both together and separately
- You examine what has brought you here, and what each of you has contributed
- The partner considering leaving gets room to say so without having to defend themselves
- The partner who wants to stay gets room to be heard without having to convince
- You land on one of three positions (see below)

Discernment counseling is especially useful when couples therapy feels impossible because one partner doesn't know whether they want to invest in the relationship at all. It's hard to work on communication when the real question is "do I want to be here?"

## Discernment counseling vs. regular couples therapy

| | Discernment counseling | Regular couples therapy |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Clarity about the relationship's future | Improve the relationship |
| **Prerequisite** | At least one partner is unsure whether to continue | Both want to work on the relationship |
| **Length** | Short and bounded, often 1–5 sessions | Often 8–20 sessions or more |
| **Format** | Joint and individual conversations | Mainly joint |
| **Therapist's role** | Help each of you understand your own position | Help you change the pattern between you |
| **Outcome** | A decision: stay, go, or try properly for a defined period | A changed relationship |
| **Fits when** | "I don't know if I want to" | "We want to, but we can't make it work" |

Many couples start with discernment and move into couples therapy if they choose to try. It's a natural path.

## The three positions: Stay, go, or try properly

Discernment counseling doesn't end in "yes" or "no". It ends in one of three positions:

**1. Stay as things are.** You choose to continue without changing anything substantial. This is rarely the outcome, but it's legitimate when the ambivalence turns out to be about something other than the relationship, such as a life crisis or your own unrest.

**2. Go.** One or both of you have concluded the relationship should end. The rest of the work is then about doing it as cleanly as possible, especially if you have children.

**3. Try properly, for a defined period.** You choose to give the relationship a real chance, with couples therapy, with clear goals and with a time frame, for example six months. After that period you reassess. This is the most common outcome, and the one that most often produces a good decision whatever it turns out to be, because you know you tried.

The point of the third position is that "try properly" is something other than "stay and hope". It's an active effort with an agreed end. That makes it possible for the unsure partner to say yes, because it isn't forever.

## When one wants to stay and the other wants to go

This is the most common constellation. And the most painful.

The partner considering leaving (often called the "leaning-out" partner) has usually been thinking for a long time. They're tired, may have finished grieving in silence, and experience the partner's sudden willingness to change as too late.

The partner who wants to stay often didn't see it coming, or didn't want to. They're in shock, in panic, and will promise anything to save it.

Both positions are understandable. And both are poor places to make decisions from. One decides from exhaustion, the other from fear.

Discernment counseling gives both a room. The one leaning out gets to say it without having to defend themselves or comfort their partner. The one who wants to stay gets to be heard without having to convince. And both get help understanding how you ended up here, so the decision is about the relationship, not about the acute crisis.

## Questions that bring clarity

Answer separately. Don't share your answers yet.

1. When did I start to doubt? What was happening around that time?
2. If I had to describe our pattern in one sentence, what would it be?
3. What have I done, or failed to do, that has contributed to where we are?
4. What would have to change for me to want to stay? Be specific.
5. Have I told my partner? Clearly, not hinted?
6. Have I given my partner a real chance to change it, or have I concluded in silence?
7. If I leave, what do I think I'll miss? What won't I miss?
8. If I stay, what am I afraid of? If I leave, what am I afraid of?
9. Is there someone in my life who has made me more unsure about the relationship? Who, and why?
10. What would I say to a good friend who described my situation?

Questions 3, 4 and 6 are the most important. If you can't answer 4, you don't yet know what you're considering leaving. If you answer no to 6, you haven't given the relationship a chance, you've given it a verdict.

## Signs the relationship can be saved

- Both of you can describe the pattern you're stuck in, not just the other's faults.
- Both can say something about their own part in it.
- There is still respect, even where there is no warmth.
- The doubt came gradually, alongside a period of stress, children, conflict or distance, not out of nowhere.
- The partner considering leaving can say what would have to change.
- You've never really tried, with help, over time.
- You both feel grief at the thought of leaving, not only relief.

If many of these apply, you're probably in position 3: try properly. Read more about what that involves in our [complete guide to couples therapy](/blog/couples-therapy-guide).

## Signs it may be right to go

- You fundamentally want different lives: children or not, where to live, lifestyle, values you can't meet on.
- There is contempt, not just conflict. Condescension, mockery, systematic devaluation.
- There is repeated betrayal without real change, despite chances and help.
- One of you has already, in practice, left: living a separate life, in another relationship, checked out.
- You've tried properly, with help, over time, without anything changing.
- You feel only relief at the thought of leaving, no grief, over a long period.
- There is violence, threats, control or substance abuse. Then the question isn't whether you should separate, but how you get out safely. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. In immediate danger, call 911.

## What happens if you choose to try

You move into regular couples therapy, with some important conditions:

- **A time frame.** For example six months. Then you evaluate.
- **Clear goals.** Not "be happier", but "break the pursue–withdraw pattern", "restore physical closeness", "be able to talk about money without fighting".
- **Full effort from both.** The one who considered leaving puts the exit on the shelf for the period. The one who wanted to stay stops monitoring whether the partner "means it".
- **Honesty about what has happened.** Including what hurts.

Many couples who take this path find their way back to each other. Some don't, but they part knowing they tried, and that's an entirely different ending.

## What happens if you choose to go

Then the rest of the work is about ending in a way you can live with.

Especially if you have children, this is decisive. How you separate shapes your children's security for years. The therapist can help you:

- Talk together about what to tell the children, and how
- Agree on a framework for co-parenting
- Handle practical questions without them becoming a new arena for conflict
- Grieve the relationship, separately and together

Legal separation and divorce processes, including any mediation requirements, vary by state and country. Discernment counseling doesn't replace legal advice or mediation, but it often makes both easier, because you arrive with a shared understanding of what happened.

## FAQ: Should we separate?

### How do I know if I should get divorced?

You rarely know by thinking more on your own. Examine three things: what has happened to the relationship, what your own part in it is, and what would have to change for you to stay. If you can't answer the last one, you don't yet know what you're considering leaving. Discernment counseling is designed to find those answers.

### What is discernment counseling?

Discernment counseling is a short, structured form of couples work, often 1–5 sessions, for couples where at least one partner is unsure whether the relationship should continue. The goal is not to save or end the relationship, but to make a clear decision: stay, go, or try properly for a defined period.

### Can couples therapy help if I've already decided to leave?

Yes, but then in the form of discernment and closure, not repair. Therapy can help you understand what happened, communicate about children and practical matters, and end in a way you can live with. Be honest with the therapist about where you stand.

### What if my partner doesn't know I'm considering leaving?

Then the first step is to say it, clearly, not hinted. It hurts, but the uncertainty is worse, and your partner deserves a real chance to respond to it. Discernment counseling can be a safe place to say it, with a third person present.

### Is it too late for couples therapy when I've been thinking about divorce for a long time?

Not necessarily. Long doubt is common, and many couples who have considered separating for years find their way back when they get help understanding the pattern. But if you've finished grieving in silence and feel only relief, that should be taken seriously. Discernment counseling helps you tell the two apart.

### How long does it take to decide?

In discernment counseling most people land on a position within 1–5 sessions. If you choose to try, you usually agree on a period of 3–6 months before reassessing. The point is not to stand in uncertainty for years.

### Should we stay together for the children?

Research suggests that what harms children most is a high level of conflict, whether the parents are together or not. A stable, low-conflict relationship is good for children. A high-conflict one isn't, even if you live together. So the question isn't "for the children's sake", but "what kind of home can we give them, together or apart?".
