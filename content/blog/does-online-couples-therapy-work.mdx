The question "does online couples therapy work" usually means something more specific: *will my partner actually engage through a screen, or will they half-watch it like a meeting?* Or: *can a therapist read us when they can't see our feet?* Or: *isn't this just a worse version of the real thing?*

Those are good questions, and the research answers the first-order version clearly. The second-order versions, about engagement and attention, are where the real work of making online therapy effective happens, and they're mostly in your control.

## Short answer: Does it work?

Yes, for most couples, with outcomes comparable to in-person therapy.

The evidence base has grown substantially since 2020 and now includes direct comparisons of video and in-office delivery of established couples therapies, large trials of structured online relationship programmes, and follow-up data showing gains hold. Across these, online couples therapy produces meaningful improvement in relationship satisfaction and communication, with effect sizes in the range of in-person work.

Where it falls short is specific and predictable: when one partner is unsafe to speak freely, when there's no private space, and when the screen becomes a way to be absent while present. Each of these can be assessed before starting, and a good practice will do so.

## What the research shows

Three strands of evidence, in order of directness.

**Direct comparisons of video and in-person couples therapy.** Studies delivering established models, including Emotionally Focused Therapy and integrative behavioural approaches, by videoconference and comparing outcomes with in-office treatment report similar improvements in relationship satisfaction, similar therapeutic alliance ratings, and similar retention. Couples do not report feeling less connected to the therapist on video once the first session or two have passed.

**Trials of structured online relationship programmes.** Large randomised trials of online couples programmes, some with coach contact and some fully self-directed, show significant improvements in relationship satisfaction, communication and individual wellbeing, with gains maintained at follow-up. These programmes are not the same as live therapy with a clinician, but they establish that the medium itself does not prevent relationship change, and they have reached populations (rural, low-income, time-poor) that clinic-based therapy consistently misses.

**The general telehealth literature.** Meta-analyses across mental health conditions find video therapy non-inferior to in-person for most presentations, with equivalent alliance and outcomes. Couples work was historically assumed to be an exception because of its interactional complexity; the direct comparison studies above have not supported that assumption.

What the research does not yet show: long-term follow-up beyond a couple of years specifically for video couples therapy, and fine-grained data on which couples do worse online. The honest summary is that online couples therapy is well supported as a format, and the exceptions are clinical judgement calls rather than research findings.

## What changed after 2020

Before 2020, online couples therapy was a niche offering and most clinicians had little experience of it. The pandemic moved essentially the entire field onto video within weeks, and several things followed.

Therapists got good at it, fast. The clumsy early adaptations gave way to developed technique: how to track two people on one screen, how to manage a couple in two locations, how to intervene in escalation when you can't physically shift your chair.

Couples normalised it. The sense that video therapy was second-best faded as it became the way therapy happened.

Research caught up. The direct comparison studies that now anchor the evidence base were largely conducted or completed in this period, because for the first time there were large numbers of couples receiving the same therapies by both routes.

And a structural fact became obvious: couples who could never have attended weekly in-person therapy, because of distance, travel, children, disability or scheduling, could attend online. Access, not equivalence, turned out to be the format's main contribution.

## What's actually different about therapy on video

Not as much as people expect, and the differences aren't all losses.

**The therapist sees you at home.** Your space, your light, the dog, the interruptions. Some of this is noise; some is information.

**Both faces are in frame at once.** On a single screen with both partners, the therapist sees both reactions simultaneously, which in a room requires looking back and forth. Micro-expressions during the other partner's speech are easier to catch.

**Escalation is handled differently.** In a room, a therapist can lean forward, shift position, use physical presence to slow things. Online, that becomes voice, pace and explicit structure. Many therapists find they become more direct and more structured online, which some couples prefer.

**There's no transition.** No drive to the office, no waiting room, no drive home. Couples go from kitchen to session to kitchen. This loses a buffer that some couples used, and gains time that others never had.

**Breaks are real.** A partner who needs a moment can step out of frame. In a room, leaving is a bigger act.

**Privacy inverts.** Nobody sees you arrive at a clinic. But your home has to be private for the hour.

## What's lost, and how good therapists compensate

Honest list.

**Physical presence.** The felt sense of three people in a room is different from three faces on a screen. Compensation: longer first sessions to build alliance, more explicit check-ins about how the format feels, and explicit agreements about pausing and returning.

**Full-body information.** Posture, fidgeting, who turns toward whom. Compensation: ask the couple to frame wide enough to see upper bodies and hands, and to sit as they naturally would, not posed for a camera.

**The therapist's ability to physically regulate the room.** Compensation: clear agreements about pausing, a stop word, and the therapist being more active in managing turns.

**Ritual.** Going somewhere for therapy marks it as different. Compensation: couples can build their own ritual around the session: a walk after, a cup of tea before, a rule that the hour after is not for logistics.

None of these is trivial, and none has shown up in outcome data as decisive. They're differences to work with, not reasons to avoid the format.

## When online is the wrong choice

This matters more than the research summary.

**Intimidation, coercion or control in the relationship.** In a therapist's office, both partners are on neutral ground and the therapist can see what happens when they walk in and out. At home, the controlling partner controls the space, and the other partner may not be able to speak. Any practice offering online couples therapy should screen for this in individual contact with each partner before the first joint session. If it's present, couples therapy in any format is contraindicated until safety is addressed.

**No private space.** If sessions would be overheard by children, housemates, parents or colleagues, one or both partners will edit themselves, and edited therapy is ineffective. This is solvable (a car, a booked room, a friend's apartment, scheduling when the house is empty) but it must be solved, not hoped around.

**Unreliable connection.** A session that drops three times isn't a session. Fixable, but fix it first.

**One partner using the screen to disengage.** See the next section.

**Active crisis requiring in-person containment.** Rare in couples work, but if one partner's state would need physical presence to manage safely, online isn't right that day.

## The disengagement problem

The most common way online couples therapy fails isn't technical. It's that one partner treats it like a video call: camera on, attention elsewhere.

Signs: a second screen just out of frame, eyes that track something other than the therapist or partner, answers that arrive a beat late, a tendency to "just check this" in a pause. The other partner always notices, and it confirms exactly what they came to therapy about.

This is worth naming before you start, with each other and with the therapist. Agreements that help: one device, nothing else open, phones in another room, the session treated as the one hour in the week with no exit. A good therapist will also name it in the room if they see it, because the disengagement is itself material.

If you know your partner is likely to half-attend in weekly sessions, consider whether an intensive format, where duration makes disengagement much harder, fits better. The format should match the couple, not the other way around.

## Both in one room, or two locations?

**One room, one screen** is the default and the better-studied configuration. The couple shares physical space; the therapist sees them together; the dynamic between them is visible. If you can be in the same place, be in the same place.

**Two locations, two screens** is the configuration that makes online couples therapy possible for long-distance couples, travelling partners and couples separated by circumstance. It works, with adjustments: sessions run longer (90 minutes) to compensate for the lost shared space, the therapist manages turns more actively, and the couple loses the ability to reach for each other physically in the session, which matters in some moments. It also gains something: each partner is in their own safe space, which some find freeing.

Both configurations are standard here. [More on two-location work in our long-distance couples article.](/blog/long-distance-commuter-couples-therapy)

## Setting up: the ten things that matter

1. **One screen if you're together.** Laptop or tablet on a table, not a phone held in a hand.
2. **Both of you fully in frame**, upper body and hands, sitting as you would on a couch.
3. **Front lighting.** A window or lamp in front of you, not behind. The therapist needs to see faces.
4. **Headphones only if you're in two locations.** Together, use the device's speaker so you can both hear and be heard naturally.
5. **A door that closes**, and everyone who might open it informed.
6. **Phones in another room.** Not face down. Elsewhere.
7. **Nothing else open** on the device.
8. **Wired or strong wifi.** Test it before the first session.
9. **Ten minutes before**, sit down, stop doing things, and arrive. The commute you don't have is a buffer you need to create.
10. **Ten minutes after**, don't go straight to logistics. A walk, a cup of something, a few minutes of quiet.

## Choosing an online couples therapist

Three things beyond the usual credentials and approach questions:

**Experience with the format.** Not "do you offer online" but "what proportion of your couples work is online, and how long have you been doing it?" Technique for video couples work is real and learned.

**Screening practice.** Do they speak to each partner individually before joint sessions start? For online work, this is how intimidation and control get caught.

**Two-location capability**, if you need it. Some practices only do single-screen. Ask.

## FAQ: Does online couples therapy work?

### Is online couples therapy as effective as in-person?

For most couples, yes. Direct comparisons of video-delivered and in-office couples therapy, including Emotionally Focused Therapy, show comparable improvements in relationship satisfaction and communication, similar therapeutic alliance, and similar retention. Large trials of online relationship programmes also show significant, maintained gains. The exceptions are specific: unsafe relationships, no private space, and partners who use the screen to disengage.

### Can a therapist really read a couple through a screen?

Yes, with some adjustments. Both faces are visible simultaneously, which can make reactions easier to track than in a room. What's lost is full-body information and physical presence; good online therapists compensate with wider framing, more explicit structure, and more active management of turns. Outcome data don't show these differences as decisive.

### When should couples not do therapy online?

When there is intimidation, coercion or control in the relationship, because the controlling partner controls the home space; when no private space is available; when the connection is unreliable; when one partner is likely to half-attend; or when an acute situation needs in-person containment. A good practice screens for the first of these before any joint session.

### Does online couples therapy work if we're in different locations?

Yes. Two-screen sessions are a standard configuration, with adjustments: 90-minute sessions, more active turn management, and the loss of physical reach between partners during the session. It's the format that makes therapy possible for long-distance and travelling couples at all.

### How do we stop my partner from checking email during online therapy?

Agree in advance: one device, nothing else open, phones in another room, the hour treated as the one with no exit. Name it with the therapist, who will address it in the room if it happens. If you know one of you will half-attend weekly sessions, consider an intensive format instead.

### Is online couples therapy private?

Nobody sees you attend, and nothing enters an employer or insurance record unless you submit for reimbursement. The requirement is on your side: a room with a closed door for the hour, and everyone who might interrupt informed. Sessions should not be recorded; ask the practice.

### What's the research on online EFT specifically?

Emotionally Focused Therapy delivered by video has been compared directly with in-person EFT, with comparable outcomes on relationship satisfaction and attachment measures and no meaningful difference in alliance. EFT's structure, which tracks the emotional cycle between partners, translates well to a format where both faces are visible at once.
