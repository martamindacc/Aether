You can explain a distributed system to a new hire in twenty minutes. You cannot explain to your partner why you're staying eighteen more months at a job you both know is hurting you, because the honest answer is a number in a brokerage app and it sounds worse out loud.

Your partner has stopped asking when the reorg will settle. They know the answer: it doesn't. They've learned not to plan anything in the weeks around performance reviews, they sleep through the pager most nights now, and they've noticed that you're most present at home on the days the company is in the news for the wrong reasons.

None of this is a fight. That's the problem.

## Short answer: What does a couples therapist for tech executives do?

A couples therapist for tech executives does standard, evidence-based couples therapy (usually Emotionally Focused Therapy, often with Gottman-derived tools) with one difference: they already understand the structures of tech work, so the job comes into the room as context instead of eating the session. Equity schedules, reorgs, performance calibration, on-call, return-to-office mandates, visa dependency and the AI-era pace all shape how tech couples fight, withdraw and make decisions. A good one is fluent in that world without being impressed by it, moves fast, and treats the partner who lives with the job as equally central to the person who does it.

It isn't always couples sessions. When the problem starts with how the tech partner carries the job (the stay-or-go decision, the reorg dread, never switching off), one-to-one coaching for that partner can unlock it, with couples work for the pattern between you. The format that fits is usually online, at early-morning or evening Pacific-time slots, with intensives when launch and review cycles defeat weekly sessions.

## Why tech is its own context

Every demanding career takes time and energy from a relationship. Tech takes them in a specific shape, and the shape is what couples run into.

**Compensation is time-locked.** A large share of a senior tech package is equity that vests over years, typically with a one-year cliff, then quarterly or monthly, then refreshed with new grants that start their own schedules. There is almost never a date when leaving is free. That turns "should I stay?" from a career question into a household one.

**Instability is a feature, not an event.** Reorgs, hiring freezes, "efficiency" years and performance-based cuts are part of the operating rhythm. In January 2025, Meta announced it would cut roughly 5% of staff, targeting its lowest-rated performers, and framed it as raising the bar. When cuts are tied to ratings, every review cycle carries a layoff question.

**Your worth gets rated on a calendar.** Self-reviews, peer feedback, calibration, promo packets. Twice a year, many tech workers watch their value be negotiated in a meeting they're not in. It comes home.

**Availability is the job.** On-call rotations, incident channels, Slack that follows you to dinner, leadership across time zones. The research on this is clear (more below): the expectation of being reachable harms recovery even on nights when nothing happens, and it reaches your partner too.

**Employment can be legal status.** For couples on H-1B and H-4 visas, losing the job starts a clock for both of you. That asymmetry sits underneath a lot of tech marriages and almost never gets said out loud.

**The pace has changed again.** Through 2025, Fortune and others reported San Francisco AI startups openly recruiting for "996" (9am to 9pm, six days a week), with one job posting asking for roughly 70 hours a week in person. In April 2026, a Menlo Park psychotherapist told the San Francisco Standard that around 80% of her patients now work on or with AI. Both partners know the mission is real. That's what makes it so hard to argue with.

Generic advice about "work-life balance" doesn't touch any of this. You don't need more balance. You need a way to make these structures discussable at home.

## The eight patterns in tech relationships

### 1. The vesting cliff: "Just one more year"

The cliff is twelve months away. Then the next tranche is big. Then the refresher lands and resets the horizon. Each step is rational. Together they produce a relationship that lives on a vest schedule, where the partner's life (moving cities, a second child, their own career change) is permanently scheduled for "after".

What the tech partner hears in their head: *I'm doing this for us.* What the other partner hears: *our life is collateral, and I wasn't consulted on the term.* Golden handcuffs are a career problem when you're single. In a couple, they're a decision about two people's years, usually made by one of them.

### 2. Reorg weather: the dread that comes home

Survivors of a layoff round don't get relief. They get a bigger scope, fewer people and the question of whether the next round includes them. Most tech leaders handle this by handling it: composed at work, composed at home, nothing to report.

Your partner absorbs it anyway. Research on married couples in a downsizing organisation found that burnout crossed over from one spouse to the other, linked to job insecurity (Westman, Etzion & Danon, 2001). Your partner picks up the dread without the information, which is the worst version: all the anxiety, none of the facts, and the sense that asking "are we okay?" would be one more thing for you to manage.

### 3. Perf season: the month nobody plans anything

Your partner knows the calendar better than you think. They know not to book the trip in late Q4. They know that the week after calibration you're either euphoric or unreachable. They've learned to read the outcome from how you close the front door.

The cost isn't the month. It's that a committee you don't control gets a vote on the emotional weather of your home twice a year, and the relationship has quietly agreed to it.

### 4. The pager in bed

On-call is the most underestimated relationship stressor in tech, because on the good weeks nothing happens. That's exactly the trap. A study of workers on extended availability found worse mood and higher stress markers the morning after on-call days, even when no call came (Dettmers et al., 2016). Separate research found that the mere *expectation* of monitoring email after hours raised anxiety in employees and in their partners, and lowered relationship satisfaction, whether or not any work was actually done (Becker, Belkin, Conroy & Tuskey, 2019).

Read that twice. It's not the hours. It's the availability. A partner who is half-listening for the phone is not fully in the room, and both of you feel it.

### 5. Debugging the partner

Engineering trains a superb reflex: find the root cause, ship the fix, close the ticket. At home, your partner brings you something hard and you're three questions into diagnosis before they've finished the sentence. You think you're helping. They experience being triaged.

The irony is that tech already has the right tool and uses it at work: the blameless postmortem. Describe the sequence, assume good intent, find the system that produced the failure. At home, most tech couples run the opposite: whose fault was it, what's the fix, ship it. The pattern is the problem, not either person. [The four conflict patterns couples get stuck in →](/blog/same-fight-four-conflict-patterns)

### 6. Two tech careers, one household

When both of you work in tech, you understand each other's days, and you share each other's risk. Same industry, same layoff cycles, sometimes the same stock, often the same city's housing costs. That is concentrated exposure, and it means the bad quarter usually arrives for both of you at once. Research on dual-earner couples found that one partner's job insecurity intensified the other's reaction to their own (Debus & Unger, 2017).

Then there's the scheduling: two on-call rotations, two review cycles, two sets of vesting dates that each argue for a different move. Whose cliff wins? [Two careers, one relationship →](/blog/two-careers-one-relationship)

### 7. The visa clock

For H-1B couples, a layoff starts a grace period of up to 60 days to find a new sponsor, change status or leave (8 CFR 214.1(l)(2)). An H-4 spouse's status, and sometimes their right to work, depends on that. A green card process tied to one employer can make leaving even a bad job a decision about where your family lives.

That creates a power asymmetry most couples never name: one partner's career decisions carry legal weight for the other, and the dependent partner often stops raising concerns because the stakes feel too high to add to. In therapy, naming this out loud is frequently the moment the relationship starts moving again. (For the legal questions themselves, an immigration attorney, not a therapist.)

### 8. Mission creep: when the work is framed as existential

It's easy to argue with a job. It's hard to argue with a mission, especially in AI right now, where the people building the systems believe the stakes are civilisational and some of them may be right. When the work is framed as history, a partner asking for Saturday sounds petty, and they know it. So they stop asking.

The tech partner isn't wrong that the work matters. The relationship still needs someone to say that a mission doesn't get every hour by default, and that "this is important" has been true of every quarter for three years.

## Total comp is not a salary: the money conversations tech couples skip

Tech money is lumpy, conditional and partly fictional until it isn't. That produces arguments that look like they're about money and are really about trust.

**Paper wealth vs. spendable money.** Unvested equity and private-company options feel like wealth and behave like a bet. One partner plans with the paper number. The other plans with the bank balance. Neither is wrong, and they're not having the same conversation.

**The April surprise.** In the US, vested RSUs are taxed as supplemental wages. Employers commonly withhold federal tax at a flat 22% on supplemental wages up to $1 million in a year, and must use 37% above that (IRS Publication 15). For many senior tech earners, 22% is below their actual marginal rate, so a big vest year can mean a large bill at filing. It's a technical issue that becomes a marital one when one partner handles the money and the other finds out in April.

**The leave-or-lose window.** Leaving a company often starts a short window to exercise stock options, commonly 90 days, after which they can lapse. That turns "I want to quit" into "I need to find a large sum of money in three months," which is a joint decision whether or not it's treated as one.

**The number.** "When we hit the number, I'll slow down." Most tech couples have a number. Very few have agreed on it, and the number tends to move when it gets close. [Money and values after liquidity →](/blog/money-and-values-after-liquidity)

None of this is tax or financial advice; that's what a CPA and a fee-only planner are for. The therapy question is different: who decides, who knows, and what the money is actually for.

## Self-check: has the job taken the relationship?

Answer honestly, both of you, separately.

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Major life decisions are scheduled around vest dates | The equity has a vote the partner doesn't |
| You plan the year around perf season and launches | The company's calendar has become the household's |
| The phone sleeps on the nightstand during on-call weeks, and sometimes when it isn't one | Availability has become the default state |
| Your partner learned about the reorg or layoff risk from someone else | Composure has turned into exclusion |
| Most conversations at home are logistics | You're running a household, not a relationship |
| One of you "handles" the money and the other doesn't really know | A trust gap is growing under the numbers |
| A visa or green card makes one partner's concerns hard to raise | There's a power asymmetry nobody has named |
| "After this quarter" has moved more than twice | The relationship lives in deferral |

## What helps: seven moves that fit a tech life

### 1. Make the vest decision a joint decision

Sit down with the actual vesting schedule. Put two columns next to it: what staying costs the relationship, what leaving costs the household. Agree a date to revisit it. The goal isn't a particular answer. It's that the decision about your shared years stops being made by one person, quietly, a quarter at a time.

### 2. Put availability on the shared calendar

On-call weeks, launch weeks, review weeks: visible to both of you in advance. Then agree what's protected inside them (dinner, bedtime, one evening). The researchers behind the after-hours email studies recommended that availability expectations be made explicit at work. Do the same at home: explicit beats ambient.

### 3. Run real postmortems on your fights

Blameless means blameless. Walk through the sequence: what happened, what each of you felt at each step, what each of you did with that feeling. No root-causing the other person. You'll find the same loop every time, and the loop is what you work on.

### 4. Ask "witness or fix?" before you solve

Six words that interrupt the debugging reflex. Most of the time the answer is "witness", and the fix you were about to ship was correct and useless.

### 5. Pre-brief perf season and reorgs

Before calibration, tell your partner what you're worried about and what outcome would hit hardest. After, tell them what actually happened, including the parts that sting. Your partner can handle bad news. What corrodes trust is reading it off your face.

### 6. Talk about correlated risk if you're both in tech

Not a financial plan, a conversation: what happens if both of us are hit in the same quarter? What would we change now? Couples who've had that talk handle a bad year as a team instead of as two people quietly panicking in adjacent rooms.

### 7. Say the asymmetry out loud

Whose job carries the visa, whose career moved for whose, whose equity dominates the net worth. Not to relitigate it. To make it speakable, so the partner with less leverage stops editing what they say.

## How couples therapy for tech executives differs

The goal isn't therapy for its own sake. It's getting the relationship off the list of things the job is quietly breaking. The methods are the standard evidence-based ones. What changes is how they're delivered to people whose jobs train exactly the habits that make couples work hard.

**Couples work, coaching, or both.** Some of the problem lives between you; some of it lives in how one of you carries the job. Couples sessions work on the first. [Founder and executive coaching](/executive-founder-work) works on the second: the decision to stay or leave, the boundaries with the company, the composure that has stopped switching off. Many tech couples use both, in parallel or in sequence.

**No onboarding tax.** You shouldn't spend three sessions explaining what an L7 is, why a refresher matters or what a SEV1 at 2am does to a weekend. A therapist fluent in the context goes straight to what it does to the two of you.

**The debugging frame gets interrupted, not adopted.** A therapist who turns your relationship into an OKR dashboard is flattering the pattern. The work is to slow the fix reflex down in the room, so the partner can be heard before being solved.

**Composure doesn't get a free pass.** Tech leaders are articulate about their own patterns. A good therapist hears the polished account and goes underneath it, through duration, specificity and asking the partner what it looks like from their side.

**The partner is not supporting cast.** The person who adapted around the job (the pager, the cliffs, the visa, the moves) is central to the work, not an audience for the executive's stress.

**The format survives your calendar.** Online by default, early-morning and evening Pacific-time slots, two-location sessions when one of you is travelling, and intensives (two days instead of months of weekly sessions) timed around launches and review cycles rather than through them. ## Five questions to ask a therapist before you book

Use the free consultation. Both of you attend. Ask:

1. **"What happens to a relationship during a reorg or a layoff round?"** You're listening for whether they understand the dread of survivors, not just the shock of the person laid off.
2. **"How would you work with one of us being on-call?"** A good answer talks about availability and recovery, not just "set boundaries".
3. **"What would you do if one of us kept trying to fix things in session?"** You want someone who'll interrupt it, kindly and early.
4. **"How do you handle it when one partner's job, equity or visa gives them more leverage?"** If they've never thought about it, that's your answer.
5. **"Do you record sessions or use AI note-taking?"** You're entitled to know where your words go, and in tech you'll care.

Watch out for the opposite failure too: a therapist so dazzled by your title that they never challenge you. Fluency, not flattery. [The full guide, with twelve questions and eight red flags →](/guides/how-to-choose-couples-therapist-founders-executives)

## Privacy in an industry where everyone knows everyone

Tech is a big industry and a small world. Your skip-level's spouse might be in the waiting room. Anonymous forums are full of people who'd recognise your org from three details.

Online couples therapy from a practice outside your city removes the waiting room, the clinic address and the recurring calendar block colleagues can see. Some tech employees also prefer not to route couples therapy through an employer program, even a confidential one; private pay keeps it entirely outside your employer. Employer wellbeing budgets that reimburse you directly are a different matter, and many Bay Area tech companies have them: ask HR how reimbursement works and what, if anything, is shared.

## FAQ: Couples therapy for tech executives

### What is a couples therapist for tech executives?

A couples therapist who uses evidence-based methods (such as Emotionally Focused Therapy and Gottman-derived tools) and already understands the structures of tech work: equity vesting, reorgs and layoffs, performance calibration, on-call, return-to-office mandates and visa dependency. That fluency means sessions go to the relationship rather than to explaining the job, and the therapist can see how those structures drive the couple's pattern.

### Does my couples therapist need to have worked in tech?

No. They need to understand your context well enough that you don't have to explain it, and they must not be impressed by it. A therapist who used to be an engineer but adopts your debugging frame is less useful than one who has never written code but interrupts the fix reflex in the first session.

### Is couples therapy for tech couples different from therapy for other high-pressure careers?

The methods are the same. The pressures are structured differently: compensation that locks you in on a schedule, layoffs as a recurring feature, performance ratings on a fixed calendar, constant availability and, for many couples, immigration status tied to one partner's employer. A therapist who knows these structures spots the pattern faster. For the broader picture, see [couples therapy for executives and high-achieving couples](/couples-therapy-executives).

### Should I stay at my job until my RSUs vest if it's hurting my marriage?

That's a decision for the two of you, and therapy won't make it for you. What therapy does is change who makes it: from one partner deciding quietly, one quarter at a time, to both of you deciding with the real costs on the table, including the ones that don't show up in a brokerage account.

### How do we fit couples therapy around on-call and launches?

Book online sessions in early-morning or evening Pacific-time slots, keep sessions on non-on-call weeks where you can, and consider an intensive timed two to four weeks before a known crunch (a launch, review season, a planning cycle) instead of during it. Two-location sessions work when one of you is travelling.

### We both work in tech in the same city. Is couples therapy really confidential?

Clinical confidentiality applies with the standard legal limits. Practically, online therapy from a practice outside your local industry removes the waiting room and the local overlap. Ask any therapist how they screen for dual relationships, whether sessions are recorded and whether they use AI note-taking. At Aether Practice, sessions are not recorded and we screen for professional overlap before starting.

### Can we use our employer's wellbeing budget for couples therapy?

Often, yes. Many Bay Area tech companies offer wellbeing or lifestyle budgets that reimburse therapy directly. Check with HR how reimbursement works and what information is shared. Private pay keeps it entirely separate from your employer. [Couples therapy cost and insurance in New York and San Francisco →](/guides/couples-therapy-cost-insurance-nyc-san-francisco)

### Is this couples therapy or coaching?

Whichever solves the problem. When the trouble lives between you (the loop you keep running, the distance, the decisions one of you makes alone), couples sessions are the tool. When it starts with how the tech partner carries the job (whether to stay through the next cliff, how to survive a reorg without bringing it home, how to set limits on availability with the company), individual [founder and executive coaching](/executive-founder-work) often moves it faster. Many tech couples do both, and the free 15-minute consultation is where you work out which.
