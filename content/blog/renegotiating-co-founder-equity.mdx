*This article is part of our guide to [co-founder conflict](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide). It is about equity between business partners. If you are married to your co-founder, equity sits inside a marital estate and the conversation is different — [we cover that separately](/blog/prenup-equity-founder-marriage).*

You agreed the split in a coffee shop, or on a walk, or over a document someone sent at midnight. It took twenty minutes. Neither of you had any evidence about what the next four years would actually require, so you used the only fair-seeming instrument available: you made it even, or close to it.

Then one of you went full-time nine months before the other. Or one raised the round single-handed. Or one has been building while the other has been, by any honest reading, somewhere else.

And you have said nothing, because there is no good way to start that sentence.

## Short answer: can you renegotiate co-founder equity?

Yes. It happens more often than founders think, and it is survivable — but only when it is framed as correcting a forecast rather than settling a score.

The distinction matters more than anything else in this article, and we call it the Forecast Frame. **An equity conversation that says "you have not earned what you hold" is an attack, and will be defended against. An equity conversation that says "we guessed before we had data, the guess was wrong, let's fix it" is a shared problem with a shared solution.**

Both versions can describe the identical situation. Only one of them produces a company that still has two founders afterwards.

## Why the original split was always going to be wrong

It helps to say this out loud to each other, because it removes the blame before the conversation starts.

You divided equity at the moment of **maximum uncertainty and minimum information**. You did not know who would still be in the building after the first pivot. You did not know which of you would turn out to be good at raising money, or which would be able to hire. You did not know that one of you would have a child, or burn out, or discover they are an operator rather than a founder.

An even split under those conditions is not a considered judgment that both contributions will be equal. It is a placeholder — the most defensible thing to do when nobody has any data. Accelerators that advise founders on this, Techstars among them, suggest revisiting the split during the vesting cliff for exactly this reason: a few months of working together produces information that week one did not have.

The error is not splitting evenly. The error is treating a placeholder as permanent.

## Legitimate reasons to reopen it — and opportunistic ones

Worth being honest with yourself before you raise it, because your co-founder will make this assessment whether or not you do.

**Legitimate:**

- **A sustained and large divergence in contribution** — not a quarter, a year or more, and not a matter of hours but of what the company actually got.
- **A change in role that both of you acknowledge.** One founder stepped back from operating by agreement. The split should reflect the new reality.
- **One founder went full-time materially earlier**, carrying the risk alone for a significant stretch.
- **A founder's departure or near-departure** that was resolved by them staying in a much reduced capacity.
- **There was never any vesting**, and one of you could walk tomorrow with a quarter of the company.

**Opportunistic:**

- You are angry about something else and equity is the available weapon.
- The company just got valuable and the split suddenly feels different in dollars than it did in percentages.
- An investor suggested it, and you are carrying their argument rather than your own.
- You want leverage in an unrelated disagreement.

The second list is not wicked, it is human. But a conversation driven by one of those will be recognised for what it is, and it will cost you the relationship without changing the cap table. If the real grievance is that [one of you has stopped pulling their weight](/blog/co-founder-not-pulling-weight), have that conversation first; the equity conversation goes much better after it than instead of it.

## What to measure, and what not to

Most equity conversations collapse because the two founders are measuring different things and neither has said which.

**Poor measures:**

- **Hours.** Easy to count and nearly meaningless. The founder who closed the partnership in two phone calls may have contributed more than the one who worked sixty hours that week.
- **Who had the idea.** Ideas are the cheapest input in a startup. Execution and persistence are what is scarce, and the original-idea argument tends to be raised by whoever is losing on every other measure.
- **Title.** Who is CEO is a decision about how the company presents itself, not a measure of contribution.

**Better measures:**

- **Risk carried, and when.** Time spent full-time without salary is the hardest currency in early-stage work. Earlier risk is worth more than later risk.
- **Irreplaceability.** If this person left tomorrow, what breaks, and could it be hired for? This is uncomfortable and it is the question investors will ask.
- **Outcomes owned.** Not activity — results. The raise that closed. The product that shipped. The customers that renewed.
- **Opportunity cost.** What each of you gave up to be here.

Write your own assessment down separately before you discuss it, and have your co-founder do the same. Comparing two written assessments is a far better conversation than two people improvising positions under pressure, and it makes it much harder for either of you to later misremember what was claimed.

## How to raise it

**Schedule it.** Never raise equity inside another argument, and never raise it spontaneously. Say that you want to talk about the split, give it a date a few days out, and say nothing substantive before then. The warning is a courtesy and it also means your co-founder arrives having thought rather than having been ambushed.

**Open with the frame, not the ask.** "We set the split before we knew anything, and I think reality has moved away from it. I'd like us to look at it together." Not: "I think I deserve more."

**Make it about the forecast, not the person.** The thing that was wrong is the guess you both made in week one. That is a shared mistake, and it is the only framing that lets both of you be right.

**Bring a proposal, not a grievance.** Specific numbers, specific reasoning, and — this is the part that signals good faith — a clear statement of what you would accept.

**Expect it to take more than one conversation.** A founder who hears an equity proposal and agrees in the same hour has usually not processed it. Build in time between the proposal and the decision.

## Alternatives to moving the percentages

Reopening the split is not the only instrument, and often not the best one.

**Introduce vesting now.** If you never had it, this is almost always the first move and it is far easier to agree to than a change in percentages, because it is forward-looking and symmetrical. Nobody loses anything they have earned.

**Re-vest from today.** Both founders agree that a portion of their holding re-vests over the next three or four years. This addresses the real worry — that someone leaves holding a stake they did not finish earning — without litigating the past.

**Adjust compensation instead.** If the imbalance is about current workload rather than accumulated ownership, salary is a cleaner and much less explosive instrument than equity.

**Use a top-up grant.** Rather than taking from one founder, issue new equity to the other. Both get diluted along with everyone else, which changes the emotional shape of the conversation entirely: nobody is being taken from.

That last one is worth considering carefully. The difference between "I'm giving you five points of mine" and "the company is issuing you five points" is small on a cap table and enormous in a relationship.

## If your co-founder refuses to discuss it

Some will not engage at all. Not argue — refuse.

Separate the two possible meanings before you react. Refusal sometimes means *I am frightened and I do not have an answer*, which looks identical from the outside to *I know I am ahead and I intend to stay there*.

A good test: ask for the conversation in a different form. "We don't have to decide anything. I'd like us both to write down how we see contribution over the last two years, and just read each other's." A founder who is frightened will usually do that. A founder who is defending a position will refuse that too.

If it is genuine refusal — no discussion, no mechanism, no third party — then you have learned something larger than the answer to the equity question. A partner who will not discuss the terms of the partnership is telling you what kind of partnership it is. At that point the question is not what the split should be. It is whether you want to spend the next four years here, and that is [a different decision](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit).

## FAQ: co-founder equity renegotiation

### Can you change a co-founder equity split after incorporation?

Yes. The mechanics depend on your structure and jurisdiction — share transfers, repurchases, new grants, or amended vesting — and your lawyer will have done all of them before. The hard part is never the paperwork. It is the conversation that has to happen first, and the framing that lets both founders be right about what they remember.

### Is it too late to add vesting to founder shares?

Almost never. Adding vesting to existing founder shares is common, your lawyer will have done it many times, and investors view it positively — many will ask for it at the first priced round anyway. It is usually the easiest equity conversation a founding team can have, because it is symmetrical and forward-looking.

### Should we involve our lawyer in the equity conversation?

Have the conversation first, then bring the lawyer the outcome. Starting with counsel turns a partnership discussion into a negotiation between parties, and you will not get it back. [Here is what a lawyer, a mediator and a co-founder coach each fix.](/blog/co-founder-mediation-vs-coaching)

### What if investors are pushing for a change to the split?

Investors sometimes raise it, and sometimes they are right. But their interest is the asset, not either of you. Work out what the two of you believe is fair before you carry an investor's position into the room, or the conversation becomes you versus your co-founder on someone else's behalf.

### How much should an earlier full-time start be worth in equity?

There is no formula, and anyone who gives you one is selling something. What matters is that both of you name what you think it is worth and why, in writing, before you negotiate.

### Should we fix a 50/50 split to break deadlock?

Usually not. Deadlock is a decision-rights problem, and moving a point of equity to solve it creates the resentment it was meant to prevent. [Domain ownership and a named tiebreaker](/blog/co-founder-5050-deadlock) fix deadlock without touching the cap table.

### Can we just leave the split as it is?

You can, and many teams do. Understand the cost: an unspoken equity grievance does not stay in the equity drawer. It leaks into every unrelated disagreement for years, and it tends to arrive eventually as a list.
