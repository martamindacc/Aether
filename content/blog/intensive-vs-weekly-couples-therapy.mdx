You've decided to get help. Good. Now you're on a website trying to work out whether to book a weekly slot or block out a weekend, and every page you read is written by someone selling one of the two.

This one isn't. We offer both, and we recommend weekly to a meaningful share of couples who come in asking about intensives. The question isn't which format is better. It's which format fits the two of you, right now.

*This article is part of our hub on [intensive couples therapy](/intensive-couples-therapy).*

## Short answer: Which should we choose?

**Choose weekly** if you have time, if neither of you is in crisis, if you need space between sessions to let things settle, or if one of you is hesitant and needs to ease in.

**Choose an intensive** if something urgent has happened, if your calendars have defeated weekly scheduling before, if you've done months of weekly therapy and stalled, or if you both want to do concentrated work and have the capacity for it.

**Choose a hybrid** if you want the breakthrough of an intensive and the consolidation of weekly, which is more couples than you'd think.

The rest of this article is how to tell which you are.

## What's actually different (and what isn't)

The methods are the same. Emotionally Focused Therapy, the Gottman approach, systemic and integrative work: all are delivered in both formats, by the same kinds of clinicians, aiming at the same outcomes. An intensive is not a different therapy. It is a different delivery schedule.

What changes with the schedule:

- **Continuity.** In weekly, each session restarts. In an intensive, the thread holds across hours.
- **Depth per unit of time.** Fifty minutes rarely gets past the presenting problem. A 90-minute block in the middle of a day can.
- **Escape routes.** In weekly, a hard moment can be deferred to next week. In an intensive, you stay in it.
- **Integration time.** Weekly gives you six days to try things at home. An intensive gives you an evening.
- **Dropout risk.** Weekly loses couples mid-course. Intensives are almost always completed.
- **Intensity.** Literally. Two days of emotional work is tiring in a way weekly isn't.

Each of these is an advantage in some situations and a cost in others. That's why the decision needs variables, not a verdict.

## The five variables that decide it

### 1. Urgency
How much damage does four more weeks of the current pattern do? If the answer is "not much," weekly is fine. If an affair was disclosed on Sunday, if one partner has said they're thinking of leaving, if conflict has escalated to a point that frightens you, waiting is itself a cost. Urgency favours intensive.

### 2. Calendar
Be honest about your history, not your intentions. Have you tried weekly and cancelled a third of the sessions? Does one of you travel? Do you live in different cities part of the time? Couples who can't reliably protect the same hour every week do better with a format they protect once. Calendar chaos favours intensive.

### 3. Pattern entrenchment
How long has the pattern been running, and how automatic is it? New or mild patterns respond to weekly work and benefit from practice between sessions. Patterns that have run for years, that you can both describe but can't interrupt, often need the sustained pressure of an intensive to break. Entrenchment favours intensive.

### 4. Motivation symmetry
Are you equally invested? If one of you is hesitant, sceptical, or only half-present, an intensive is a big ask. Weekly lets a reluctant partner ease in and build trust in the process. Asymmetry favours weekly. Strong shared motivation favours intensive.

### 5. Processing style
Some people need time to let an insight settle before they can act on it. Others lose it if they don't act immediately. Some find sustained emotional work energising; others find it depleting. If either of you is a slow processor or easily flooded, weekly's gaps are a feature. If you're both the type who'd rather go deep once than shallow fifteen times, intensive fits.

## The decision tree

Answer in order.

**Is there violence, threat, coercion, active substance misuse, or an acute psychiatric crisis in the relationship?**
→ Yes: neither format yet. Safety and individual help first. See "When neither is right yet."
→ No: continue.

**Is an affair ongoing, undisclosed, or disclosed within the last few days?**
→ Ongoing or undisclosed: individual work first; couples work can't proceed with an active secret.
→ Disclosed days ago: wait for stabilisation, then intensive is often right. See [Intensives for crisis](/blog/couples-intensive-crisis-infidelity-separation).
→ No affair: continue.

**Are you both willing to be in the room, in every sense, for two full days?**
→ One of you isn't: weekly, or individual sessions for the willing partner first.
→ Both yes: continue.

**Has something happened that makes four weeks of waiting costly?**
→ Yes: intensive.
→ No: continue.

**Have you tried weekly therapy and either cancelled frequently or stalled after several months?**
→ Yes: intensive, or hybrid (intensive to break through, weekly after).
→ No: continue.

**Do your calendars realistically allow the same protected hour every week for three to six months?**
→ No: intensive or split intensive.
→ Yes: continue.

**Is either of you a slow processor, or easily overwhelmed by sustained emotional work?**
→ Yes: weekly, or split intensive with integration time between days.
→ No: either format works. Choose by preference. Many couples in this position choose hybrid.

## What the research says, honestly

Most sites on this topic overclaim. Here's the actual picture.

**Weekly couples therapy** is well established. Meta-analyses across decades show large effects on relationship satisfaction, with gains that hold at follow-up for a majority of couples. Emotionally Focused Therapy in particular has strong evidence, including two-year follow-up data showing stability of gains.

**Intensive and condensed formats** have a smaller but growing evidence base. Studies of multi-day, retreat-style and compressed couples programmes report outcomes comparable to standard weekly delivery, with faster onset of change and notably lower dropout. Some early work comparing delivery schedules found the more concentrated format performed at least as well. But the studies are fewer, often smaller, and vary widely in what "intensive" means.

**What can be said with confidence:** there is no evidence that intensives are less effective than weekly. There is reasonable evidence they are comparably effective, faster, and completed more reliably. There is consistent indication that follow-up sessions after an intensive matter for maintaining gains.

**What cannot be said:** that intensives are proven superior across the board. Anyone telling you that is selling.

The practical reading: if an intensive fits your situation on the five variables, you are not trading effectiveness for convenience. If weekly fits, you are choosing the better-evidenced path and losing nothing.

## The real cost comparison

Per-booking, intensives look expensive. Per-outcome, they often aren't.

A full weekly course, 12–20 sessions over three to six months, costs the sum of those sessions plus whatever you count for the time, travel and the childcare attached to each. A two-day intensive is one package price that typically covers the pre-consultation, 12–14 hours of work and two or three follow-ups.

Across the market, two-day intensives run roughly US$2,500–7,500 or 25,000–75,000 NOK, depending on clinician and location. A 16-session weekly course at typical private rates lands in a similar or higher range once you count it honestly, and takes four to six months to deliver.

Two things the per-session comparison hides:

- **Overlap.** Weekly sessions spend a meaningful share of their minutes re-establishing context. The intensive spends almost none.
- **Non-completion.** A weekly course you abandon at session seven cost seven sessions and delivered a fraction of the outcome. See the next section.

None of this means intensives are cheaper for everyone. It means "per session" is the wrong unit. Compare per completed course.

## Completion rates: the number nobody quotes

Weekly couples therapy has a dropout problem that practices rarely advertise. Studies of couples therapy attrition commonly find that a quarter to a half of couples end before the agreed course is complete, often mid-way, often when things get hard, often because life intervenes.

Intensives are almost always completed. You've booked the weekend, arranged the childcare, cleared the calendar. The structure makes finishing the default rather than the achievement.

For couples with a history of starting and stopping, whether therapy, conversations or anything else that gets hard, this alone can be the deciding factor.

## Hybrid formats: you don't have to choose

The best fit for many couples is neither pure format.

**Intensive first, weekly after.** A two-day intensive to break the pattern and reach each other, followed by fortnightly or weekly sessions for a few months to consolidate in ordinary life. Breakthrough plus integration.

**Weekly first, intensive booster.** Months of weekly work that has stalled, followed by a one- or two-day intensive to get unstuck, then a return to occasional sessions.

**Split intensive.** Two one-day blocks a few weeks apart. Sustained depth with integration time in between, for couples who need to let things settle or can't manage consecutive days.

**Intensive with maintenance.** A two-day intensive, follow-ups, then a booster day annually or before a known pressure point. Common among founder and executive couples.

If you're torn, ask the practice about hybrids. Any practice that only offers one format will recommend that format.

## When weekly is clearly right

- You have time and no urgency
- One of you is hesitant and needs to build trust in the process
- Either of you processes slowly or floods easily
- The pattern is new or mild
- You want to practise between sessions and bring results back
- Budget is tight and spreading cost matters
- You've never done therapy and want to start gently

## When an intensive is clearly right

- Something has happened and waiting costs you
- Your calendars have beaten weekly before
- You've stalled after months of weekly work
- You both want to do concentrated, serious work
- You live far from a therapist you want to work with
- You travel, or live between places
- You want to decide, stay or go, with clarity
- Preparing for a major transition with a deadline

## When neither is right yet

Couples therapy in any format assumes two people who can speak freely and safely in the same room.

It is not the right first step when there is violence, threat, coercion or control; when one partner is in acute psychiatric crisis; when active substance misuse makes consistent participation impossible; or when an affair is ongoing or undisclosed. In these situations, individual help and, where relevant, safety come first. A responsible practice will say this in the consultation rather than book you anyway.

## FAQ: Intensive vs weekly couples therapy

### Is intensive couples therapy better than weekly?

Neither is universally better. Weekly is better-researched and suits couples who need integration time or where one partner is hesitant. Intensives suit crisis, impossible calendars and stalled therapy, with comparable outcomes, faster change and far lower dropout in the available research. The right choice depends on urgency, calendar, how entrenched the pattern is, motivation symmetry and processing style.

### Does intensive couples therapy actually work?

The evidence base is smaller than for weekly therapy but consistent: outcomes comparable to weekly delivery, faster onset of change, and much higher completion. Follow-up sessions after the intensive appear important for maintaining gains. There is no evidence intensives are less effective; there is not enough to call them proven superior.

### How much does an intensive cost compared to weekly therapy?

Two-day intensives typically run US$2,500–7,500 (25,000–75,000 NOK) as a package. A full weekly course of 12–20 sessions often costs a similar or higher total over three to six months, and a quarter to a half of weekly courses are never completed. Compare per completed course, not per session.

### Can we do an intensive and then switch to weekly?

Yes, and this hybrid is often the best option: an intensive to break the pattern, then fortnightly or weekly sessions to consolidate in daily life. The reverse also works, with an intensive booster when weekly work stalls.

### What if one of us wants an intensive and the other doesn't?

Don't book the intensive. It requires both partners fully present for two days, and a reluctant partner makes that unlikely. Start with weekly, or with individual sessions for the willing partner, and revisit. Many hesitant partners agree to an intensive once they've experienced that the therapist doesn't take sides.

### How quickly do you see results from an intensive?

Most couples describe a shift during the intensive itself, often on day two. Whether it holds depends on the follow-ups and what you do at home. Weekly therapy typically shows change over six to twelve sessions.

### Is an intensive too much for us emotionally?

It's demanding. Two days of sustained emotional work is tiring, and some people flood or need time to process. If that describes either of you, weekly or a split intensive (two single days a few weeks apart) is a better fit. A good consultation will assess this with you.
