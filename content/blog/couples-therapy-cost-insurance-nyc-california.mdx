The first question most couples ask about therapy isn't "will it work." It's "what will it cost," and the second is "will insurance cover it." Both are harder to answer than they should be, because most practices list a fee and say "superbills available" and leave you to work out the rest.

This guide works out the rest.

## Short answer: What does couples therapy cost?

In New York City and California, expect:

- **US$150–250 per session** for master's-level clinicians (LMFT, LCSW, LMHC/LPC) in group practices or outside the most expensive neighbourhoods
- **US$250–350** for experienced couples specialists, psychologists, and certified EFT or Gottman therapists
- **US$350–450+** for senior specialists in Manhattan, San Francisco or Los Angeles, longer sessions (75–90 minutes), and practices focused on executive or founder couples
- **US$2,500–7,500** for a two-day intensive, priced as a package including follow-ups

A standard course of 12–20 weekly sessions therefore totals **US$3,000–9,000** at the mid-range. That's the number to hold in mind, not the per-session fee.

Insurance usually does not cover couples therapy directly. Where your plan has out-of-network mental health benefits, you may recover 50–80% of an allowed amount after your deductible, which can bring effective cost down substantially. Details below.

## The price ranges, honestly

Published ranges for couples therapy are often misleading because they blend individual-therapy fees, directory averages and national data. For couples specifically, in these two markets, as of 2026:

|---|---|---|---|
| Group practice, master's-level | $150–250 | $150–250 | Often the only tier with in-network options |

Published sliding scales at NYC group practices run roughly $185–260. Medicare's national allowance for a 60-minute psychotherapy session in 2026 is around $167, which is a useful floor for what the system considers an hour of therapy to be worth, not a guide to private practice rates.

## What drives the price

**Credentials and certifications.** Doctoral training and method-specific certification (ICEEFT, Gottman) command a premium. Whether they're worth it depends on fit more than letters; [see our guide on choosing a therapist](/guides/how-to-choose-couples-therapist-founders-executives).

**Specialisation.** A therapist whose practice is mostly couples, or mostly a specific kind of couple, charges more than a generalist who also sees couples. Usually worth it.

**Session length.** Couples sessions are often 60, 75 or 90 minutes rather than the 50-minute individual standard. Compare per-minute, not per-session.

**Location and overhead.** Manhattan and San Francisco rents show up in fees. Online-only practices may be lower, or may not be, since you're paying for the clinician, not the room.

**Format.** Intensives cost more per booking and often less per outcome. Evening and weekend slots sometimes carry a premium.

**Experience.** Years in practice, caseload history, reputation.

**Demand.** The best couples therapists in these markets have waitlists and price accordingly.

## Why most couples therapists don't take insurance

This surprises people. The reasons are structural.

**Insurance doesn't recognise "the relationship" as a patient.** Health insurance pays for the treatment of a diagnosed condition in an individual. A couple is not an individual and relationship distress is not a billable diagnosis under most plans. So couples therapy as couples therapy isn't a covered service.

**Reimbursement rates are low and administrative burden is high.** In-network rates for the couples/family code are often well below private fees, and the paperwork, pre-authorisations and clawback risk consume clinician time.

**Diagnosis requirements create ethical problems.** To bill insurance, one partner must be designated the "identified patient" and given a mental health diagnosis. Many couples therapists are unwilling to put a diagnosis on a partner who doesn't warrant one just to unlock coverage. More on this below.

**Specialists can fill their practices without it.** In NYC and the Bay Area, demand for good couples therapists exceeds supply. Private-pay is sustainable.

The result: the therapists most likely to be in-network are generalists in group practices; the therapists most likely to be couples specialists are private-pay. That's a real trade-off, and it's worth being clear-eyed about.

## How insurance covers couples therapy when it does: CPT 90847 and the diagnosis problem

When couples therapy is covered, it's billed under **CPT 90847**, "family psychotherapy with patient present." The mechanics:

1. One partner is designated the identified patient.
2. That partner receives a mental health diagnosis (depression, anxiety, PTSD, adjustment disorder are common) that justifies "medical necessity."
3. The other partner attends as part of the identified patient's treatment.
4. The insurer covers sessions as treatment for the identified patient's diagnosis.

Three implications most practices don't spell out:

**A diagnosis enters one partner's medical record.** Permanently. It can be relevant to life insurance applications, some employment contexts and future care. For many couples this is acceptable; for some, particularly public-facing professionals, it's a real cost.

**The therapy is framed around one partner's condition.** Clinically, good therapists work with the relationship regardless. But the paperwork says one of you is the patient and the other is a collateral participant.

**Relationship-only diagnosis codes often aren't covered.** The code for "relationship distress with spouse or intimate partner" (Z63.0) exists, but many plans don't reimburse it. If neither partner has a billable condition, there's no coverage, even in-network.

If you want insurance to cover couples therapy, ask the practice how they handle the identified-patient question before you start. A good one will explain the trade-off rather than just do it.

## In-network vs out-of-network: the real difference

**In-network:** the therapist has a contract with your insurer. You pay a copay or coinsurance; the therapist bills the insurer. Simpler, cheaper per session, but the pool of in-network couples specialists in NYC and California is small and often waitlisted, and the diagnosis requirement applies.

**Out-of-network (OON):** the therapist has no contract with your insurer. You pay the full fee upfront. If your plan has OON mental health benefits, you submit a superbill and the insurer reimburses you a percentage of their "allowed amount" after you've met your OON deductible.

The phrase that matters is **"allowed amount."** This is what your insurer deems a reasonable fee for the service, and it is often lower than what the therapist charges. If the therapist charges $300 and the insurer's allowed amount is $200, and your plan reimburses 70% OON, you get $140 back, not $210. Effective cost: $160 per session, after deductible.

Still a significant saving. Just not the saving the headline percentage suggests.

Some plans have **no OON benefit at all**, particularly HMO and EPO plans. Many PPO plans do. Check before assuming.

## Out-of-network reimbursement: how superbills actually work

A superbill is an itemised receipt formatted for insurance claims. It is not a bill you owe; it's documentation you submit to get money back.

**What a valid superbill must contain:**
- Your name, date of birth, and insurance details
- The provider's name, credentials, licence number, NPI (National Provider Identifier) and tax ID/EIN
- Date and length of each session
- CPT code (90847 for couples/family; 90791 for the initial assessment)
- A diagnosis code (ICD-10) for the identified patient
- Fee charged and amount paid

**The process:**
1. You pay the therapist in full.
2. The therapist provides a superbill (per session, or monthly).
3. You submit it to your insurer via their portal, app, mail or fax.
4. The insurer processes the claim against your OON deductible and benefit.
5. Once the deductible is met, they reimburse you directly, usually by check or deposit, typically within 2–6 weeks.

**The catches:**
- Nothing is reimbursed until you've met the OON deductible, which is often $1,000–5,000 and separate from your in-network deductible.
- Reimbursement is a percentage of the allowed amount, not the fee.
- Some plans cap the number of sessions per year.
- Some plans require pre-authorisation even for OON mental health.
- The diagnosis requirement applies: a superbill without a diagnosis code will be rejected.
- Claims can be denied for coding or documentation errors. Resubmission is tedious but usually works.

## The five questions to ask your insurer

Call the member services number on your card and ask these, in this order. Write down the answers and the representative's name.

1. **Does my plan include out-of-network benefits for outpatient mental health, including telehealth?**
2. **What is my out-of-network deductible for mental health, and how much of it have I met this year?**
3. **What percentage of the allowed amount does my plan reimburse for CPT code 90847, and what is the allowed amount for that code in my area?**
4. **Is there a limit on the number of sessions per year, and is pre-authorisation required?**
5. **How do I submit a superbill, and what's the typical processing time?**

If the answer to question 1 is no, you're private-pay regardless, and the rest doesn't matter. If it's yes, questions 2 and 3 tell you your effective cost.

## The automated superbill services

Several services now handle OON claims for a fee: Mentaya, Thrizer and Reimbursify are the most common. They check your benefits, submit superbills, chase denials and in some cases advance the reimbursement so you pay only the net cost at the time of the session.

Fees run roughly $30–50 per month or a percentage of reimbursement. For couples doing weekly therapy with meaningful OON benefits, the time saved and the reduced denial rate often justify the cost. Ask your practice whether they work with one; many do.

## HSA and FSA: what's eligible and what isn't

Health Savings Accounts and Flexible Spending Accounts let you pay qualified medical expenses with pre-tax money, reducing effective cost by your marginal tax rate.

**Psychotherapy is a qualified medical expense** under IRS Publication 502, and most practices accept HSA/FSA cards directly.

**The couples-therapy caveat:** IRS rules cover expenses for the diagnosis, treatment or prevention of a medical condition. Couples therapy framed purely as relationship enhancement, with no diagnosed condition, sits in a grey area that some administrators question. In practice, most HSA/FSA payments for couples therapy go through without issue, particularly when billed under 90847 with a diagnosis. But:

- Keep your receipts and superbills in case of audit.
- If your administrator asks for substantiation, a letter of medical necessity from the therapist usually resolves it.
- Confirm with your specific administrator if you want certainty before relying on it.

**Intensives:** generally eligible on the same basis, but a large single charge is more likely to trigger a substantiation request. Ask your administrator in advance.

**Tax deduction:** out-of-pocket medical expenses, including therapy, may be deductible if they exceed a percentage of your adjusted gross income. Most people don't reach the threshold. A tax professional can tell you.

## The Good Faith Estimate: your right under the No Surprises Act

Since 2022, under the federal No Surprises Act, any provider treating a patient who is uninsured or not using insurance (which includes everyone paying privately for couples therapy) must give a written **Good Faith Estimate (GFE)** of expected costs before care begins, or within set deadlines after scheduling.

**What it includes:** the services expected, the CPT codes, the fee per service, and an estimate of total cost for the expected course (often given per session with an estimated range of sessions).

**Your rights:**
- You can request a GFE before scheduling, from any provider.
- You should receive it in writing at least one business day before a scheduled service (three days if scheduled further out).
- If you're billed **$400 or more above** the GFE, you can initiate a dispute through the federal process.
- Keep a copy.

**What it doesn't mean:** the GFE is an estimate, not a cap or a contract, and couples therapy length is genuinely unpredictable. A GFE that says "12–20 sessions at $300" is doing its job even if you end up at 24. The dispute right is for bills that exceed the estimate for the *services listed*, not for needing more sessions than expected.

A practice that provides the GFE without being asked is following the law. One that doesn't know what it is should be a concern.

## Sliding scales, packages and intensives

**Sliding scales.** Some practices reserve a portion of slots at reduced fees based on income. Group practices in NYC publish ranges like $185–260. Independent specialists sometimes have one or two sliding-scale slots; ask, don't assume.

**Packages.** Pre-paid blocks of sessions at a discount (e.g. 10 sessions for the price of 9). Useful if you're committed to a course; less so if you're testing fit.

**Intensives.** Priced as packages including pre-consultation, the intensive days and follow-ups. US$2,500–7,500 for two days is the market range. Per booking it's the most expensive option; per completed course it's often comparable or cheaper, with much higher completion rates. [Full comparison here.](/blog/intensive-vs-weekly-couples-therapy)

**Employer wellbeing budgets.** Increasingly common, especially at tech companies. Some reimburse therapy outside insurance with a receipt. Ask HR; it's often more generous than the health plan.

**EAPs.** Employee Assistance Programmes typically offer a handful of free sessions, usually with a generalist. Useful as a start, rarely sufficient for couples work, and the therapist pool is narrow.

## Total cost, not session cost: how to compare properly

Most couples compare per-session fees. That's the wrong unit.

**What actually determines what you spend:**
- Fee per session
- Sessions needed to reach your goals
- Completion: whether you finish the course or abandon it at session seven
- Reimbursement: what comes back, after what deductible
- Time and logistics: travel, childcare, lost work hours

**Worked example (illustrative).** Two options for a couple in Brooklyn:

*Option A:* in-network generalist at a $40 copay, 50-minute sessions, waitlist of 6 weeks, limited couples training. Likely course: 20+ sessions, with meaningful risk of stalling. Direct cost: ~$800+. Hidden cost: the therapy may not work, and a diagnosis enters one record.

*Option B:* private-pay couples specialist at $300, 60-minute sessions, start next week, OON plan reimbursing 70% of a $220 allowed amount after a $2,000 deductible already partly met. Likely course: 12–16 sessions. Direct cost: $3,600–4,800 upfront, with ~$1,500–2,200 back. Net: ~$2,100–2,600.

*Option C:* two-day intensive at $4,500 including follow-ups, no waitlist, typically replacing 3–6 months of weekly. OON reimbursement possible for the session components. Net: roughly $3,000–4,500.

None of these is right for everyone. The point is that the $40 copay option is not obviously the cheapest once outcome and completion are counted, and the $4,500 intensive is not obviously the most expensive.

## FAQ: Couples therapy cost and insurance

### How much does couples therapy cost in NYC?

US$150–450 per session, depending on credentials, specialisation and location. Master's-level clinicians in group practices run $150–250 (some in-network); experienced couples specialists and certified EFT/Gottman therapists $250–400; senior specialists in Manhattan with executive focus $350–450+. A 12–20 session course typically totals $3,000–9,000 before any reimbursement.

### How much does couples therapy cost in California?

Similar to NYC: US$150–450 per session, with the Bay Area and Los Angeles at the upper end. Two-day intensives run US$2,500–7,500 as packages. Online-only practices are not necessarily cheaper, since you're paying for the clinician rather than the office.

### Does insurance cover couples therapy?

Rarely as couples therapy. When covered, it's billed as family psychotherapy (CPT 90847) with one partner designated the patient and carrying a mental health diagnosis. Relationship-only diagnosis codes are often not reimbursed. Most couples specialists in NYC and California are private-pay; out-of-network benefits, where your plan has them, can return 50–80% of an allowed amount after a deductible.

### What is a superbill for couples therapy?

An itemised receipt formatted for out-of-network insurance claims, containing your details, the provider's NPI and tax ID, session dates, CPT code (90847) and a diagnosis code. You pay the therapist in full, submit the superbill to your insurer, and receive partial reimbursement after your OON deductible is met. It requires a diagnosis for one partner to be valid.

### Can I use my HSA or FSA for couples therapy?

Usually yes. Psychotherapy is a qualified medical expense, and most practices accept HSA/FSA cards. Couples therapy without any diagnosed condition sits in a grey area some administrators question; keep receipts, and a letter of medical necessity from the therapist typically resolves any substantiation request. Confirm with your administrator if you want certainty.

### What is a Good Faith Estimate for therapy?

A written estimate of expected costs that any provider must give private-pay patients under the federal No Surprises Act, before care begins. It lists services, codes, fees and an estimated total. If you're billed $400 or more above the estimate for the listed services, you can dispute it federally. It's an estimate, not a cap; needing more sessions than estimated isn't a violation.

### Is it cheaper to use an in-network therapist for couples therapy?

Per session, yes. In total, not necessarily. In-network couples options in NYC and California are mostly generalists with waitlists, courses tend to run longer, completion rates are lower, and a diagnosis enters one partner's record. A private-pay specialist with OON reimbursement, or an intensive, is often comparable in net cost with a higher chance of actually working. Compare total cost per completed course.

### Why do couples therapists charge more than individual therapists?

Longer sessions (60–90 minutes vs 50), specialised training that individual-therapy credentials don't cover, higher clinical complexity with two people in the room, and lower insurance participation meaning fees aren't subsidised. In NYC and California the premium is typically 20–50% over the same clinician's individual rate.
