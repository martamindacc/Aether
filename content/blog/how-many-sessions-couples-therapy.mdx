## Quick Answer

| Situation | Typical course |
|---|---|
| Preventive / contained issue | 4–8 sessions |
| Communication problems, entrenched patterns | 8–16 sessions |
| Emotional distance over time | 12–20 sessions |
| Infidelity or serious breach of trust | 16–24+ sessions |
| Long-standing conflict with complex patterns | Varies, assessed as you go |

This is a practical framework — not a clinical prescription. Length is determined as therapy progresses, not set in advance.

## What Research Actually Tells Us

Research on couples therapy offers useful insight — but it should be read with caution.

A study from clinical practice in the US (PubMed, 2025) found that the average number of sessions in couples therapy was around 11–12. But the range was wide: from 2 to over 26. The important question isn't the average — it's what drives the variation.

For emotionally focused couples therapy (EFT) specifically, a meta-analysis (Johnson, 2003) found that a majority of couples who completed treatment experienced significant improvement — but this applies to couples who completed a defined EFT protocol under controlled conditions. That's not the same as what a random couple starting therapy can expect.

**What research can't tell you directly:**

Research distinguishes between therapy models (EFT, Gottman, IBCT), between clinical trials and everyday practice, and between couples who complete treatment and those who drop out. Findings from one study don't automatically apply to a different course of therapy, a different therapist, or a different situation.

That doesn't mean research is useless. It means that "research says you need X sessions" almost always oversimplifies reality.

## What Determines Length for You?

**What you're working on**

[Communication problems](/blog/couples-communication-problems) that are relatively recent often respond faster to treatment than trust breaches that have been going on for years. [Infidelity](/blog/couples-therapy-after-infidelity) requires a different process than entrenched conflicts about daily life.

**How long the problem has been going on**

A pattern that's existed for 8 years is more automatic than one that started 8 months ago. More automatic patterns take longer to change.

**Both partners' motivation**

Couples therapy produces the best results when both partners are genuinely engaged. Therapy driven by one person against the other's resistance rarely produces lasting change.

**Whether one partner has individual challenges**

Depression, anxiety, or trauma in one partner affects the pace. Some courses of therapy require individual therapy to happen in parallel.

**The therapy model and therapist**

Different approaches have different typical timelines. EFT, the Gottman Method, and IBCT are all evidence-based — but they work differently and on different time horizons.

## How Often Should You Go to Couples Therapy?

Weekly is most common at the start. It builds momentum and reduces the chance that patterns revert between sessions.

Every other week works for many couples after the initial active phase, or when weekly sessions are difficult to schedule around life.

Longer gaps in the middle of an active course of therapy can reduce effectiveness — especially early on, when new patterns aren't yet stable.

Can you take a break? Yes. But a good therapist will help you plan pauses so they don't disrupt work you're in the middle of.

Intensive couples therapy — multiple sessions concentrated over a short period — is used in some contexts, particularly after acute crises like infidelity. There's some research supporting this, but it's not the standard model.

## What Happens in the First Session?

The first session of couples therapy is not therapy in the traditional sense. It's assessment.

The therapist is trying to understand:

* What's bringing you in right now
* What each of you is experiencing and wants
* What patterns are already visible in the room
* Whether couples therapy is the right fit — or whether something else should happen first

You don't need to have all the answers. You don't need to agree on what the problem is. It's actually useful information for the therapist that you see the situation differently.

After sessions 2–4, most couples begin to see their own pattern from the outside for the first time. Not because everything is resolved — but because someone has helped them name what's happening.

After sessions 6–10, it's common to begin the deeper work: what's driving the pattern, which needs aren't being met, what's actually happening beneath the surface of the conflict.

## When Do You Start Seeing Results?

Progress in couples therapy is rarely dramatic or sudden. It's usually cumulative and quiet.

**Early signs — can appear after a few sessions:**

* Conversations don't escalate as quickly as before
* You start to see the pattern while it's happening — not just afterward
* One of you tries something different, and the other receives it

**Midway signs:**

* The conflicts have the same themes, but lower intensity
* You repair more quickly after an argument
* It's easier to hear your partner's perspective without becoming defensive

**Longer term:**

* New patterns start to feel more natural
* Falling back into old patterns still happens — but the episodes are shorter and easier to interrupt

Research shows that positive changes from evidence-based couples therapy typically hold at follow-up months after treatment ends (Beasley & Ager, 2019). But that assumes the work was completed — not ended early.

## What If Couples Therapy Isn't Working?

Lack of progress doesn't automatically mean the relationship is hopeless. It can mean several things:

**The therapist or model isn't the right fit.** The relationship between therapist and couple matters enormously. It's legitimate to switch if things aren't working after 4–6 sessions.

**Goals are unclear.** Some couples start therapy without having talked about what they actually want to achieve. Vague goals produce vague results.

**One partner isn't genuinely engaged.** Therapy driven by one person against the other's resistance rarely changes the underlying patterns.

**Individual issues require individual treatment.** Sometimes the right answer is that one or both partners should be in individual therapy — either instead of or alongside couples therapy.

**The goal has shifted.** Some couples begin therapy hoping to repair the relationship and end up deciding to separate. A good therapist helps them do that well too.

**When should you consider switching therapists?**

* You don't feel seen or heard by your therapist
* The therapist is clearly taking one partner's side
* Six to eight sessions have passed without any meaningful movement
* You're dreading sessions — not because the topics are hard, but because the room doesn't feel safe

## When Do You Know You Can Stop?

There's no single right ending. But some signs point toward being ready:

* The original goals have largely been met
* You handle disagreements without them escalating into the old patterns
* Both of you feel secure enough to continue without weekly support
* You and your therapist have agreed on a plan for what to do if the patterns resurface

Ending couples therapy doesn't mean the relationship is perfect. It means you have enough tools and understanding to handle what comes next.

## Do You Have to Commit to 20 Sessions Upfront?

No. Most couples start with a shorter initial course — typically 4–6 sessions — and evaluate from there.

That's actually how it should be. Couples therapy isn't a package. Length should be assessed as you go based on what's happening in the room, not locked in blindly in advance.

A good therapist will talk with you about progress along the way and help you decide whether to continue, scale back, or wrap up.

## FAQ: Sessions in Couples Therapy

### How many sessions is typical in couples therapy?

It varies considerably. Many courses in clinical practice are 8–16 sessions, but some couples need fewer and others need more. There's no single right number.

### How long is a couples therapy session?

Most sessions are 60 minutes. Some therapists offer 90-minute sessions, particularly at the start or for more complex situations.

### How long does a full course of couples therapy take?

A typical course spans several months. Weekly sessions over 3–4 months is common. Longer courses of 6–12 months occur in more complex situations.

### How often should you go to couples therapy?

Weekly is most common in the active phase. Every other week works for many once the initial patterns have been addressed. Frequency should be adjusted to where you are in the process.

### Is 5 sessions of couples therapy enough?

For some couples with a contained and relatively recent problem: yes. For couples with long-standing patterns or serious trust breaches: rarely enough for lasting change.

### Is 10 sessions of couples therapy enough?

For many couples, 10 sessions is a solid starting point for understanding the patterns and beginning to change them. Whether it's enough depends on what you're working on.

### When does couples therapy start working?

Many couples notice the first signs of change after 3–5 sessions — often that conversations escalate a little more slowly, or that they recognize the pattern a little earlier. Lasting change takes longer.

### What happens if couples therapy isn't working?

It may mean the therapist isn't the right fit, that goals are unclear, that one partner isn't sufficiently engaged, or that individual issues need separate treatment. It doesn't necessarily mean the relationship is beyond repair.

### Can you stop couples therapy at any time?

Yes. But an incomplete course rarely produces the same results as a completed one. Talk to your therapist if you're considering stopping.

### Can you do couples therapy alone?

Yes — individual therapy to work on relationship patterns is possible and sometimes the right approach. It's not the same as couples therapy, but it can be a good alternative or supplement.

### Can you go every other week?

Yes, particularly after the initial active phase. Weekly frequency is recommended at the start to build momentum.

### How long should you try couples therapy before giving up?

There's no fixed answer. If nothing meaningful has shifted after 6–8 sessions, that's worth raising with your therapist — either to adjust the approach or consider whether something else is a better fit.

### Is couples therapy worth it?

For couples where it works: yes, and research supports the outcomes. For couples where something isn't adjusted when it's not working: less so. Ongoing evaluation matters.

### Can you do intensive couples therapy over a short period?

Intensive formats exist, particularly used after crises like infidelity. It's not the standard model, but some therapists offer it.
