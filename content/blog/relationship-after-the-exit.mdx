The wire cleared on a Tuesday. There was champagne. There were texts from people you hadn't heard from in years. For about a week it felt like everything you'd both sacrificed had been worth it.

Then the founder woke up on a Monday with nothing to do, and didn't know who they were. And the partner, who had held the household, the children, the social life, and their own career on pause through three funding rounds and two near-death experiences, looked at this person on the couch and thought: *I waited six years for this?*

Nobody tells you that the exit is a relationship event. Everyone treats it as a financial one.

## Short answer: Why do marriages struggle after an exit?

Because the exit removes the thing that was organising both of your lives, and replaces it with nothing.

For the founder, the company was identity, structure, purpose, community and status, all at once. Losing it, even voluntarily and profitably, is a loss, and losses are grieved. But nobody grants permission to grieve a success, so the founder grieves alone, often without recognising it as grief.

For the partner, the company was the reason for every sacrifice, and the exit was the promised reward. When the reward turns out to be a disoriented spouse and a marriage that has forgotten how to function without the company as its central subject, the disappointment is sharp and hard to voice.

Add real money, with all the values conflicts it surfaces, and you have a marriage under more strain than during the hardest months of the build.

## The exit nobody warns you about

There is a pattern in how founder exits are discussed. Before: strategy, valuation, terms. During: the deal, the earn-out, the lock-up. After: the next thing, the angel investing, the sabbatical.

What is almost never discussed is the eighteen months in between, where the founder has stopped being a founder and hasn't become anything else, and the marriage has to absorb that.

Research on major life transitions, including retirement, redundancy, and the end of careers in sport and the military, consistently finds a period of identity disruption, loss of purpose and strain on close relationships. Founder exits share all of those features, with two aggravations: they happen young, often in your thirties or forties, and they come wrapped in a congratulations that makes the distress feel illegitimate.

## What the founder goes through

**Identity loss.** "I'm the founder of X" was the answer to "who are you?" for years. Now it's "I used to be." The sentence hasn't finished.

**Structure collapse.** No standup, no board cycle, no inbox that matters. Many founders describe the first months as a kind of vertigo: all the time in the world and no reason to get up.

**Grief disguised as restlessness.** The urge to immediately start something new is often grief avoidance. The new thing is a way to not feel the loss of the old one.

**Status recalibration.** In rooms where you used to be the CEO, you're now "the one who sold." Sometimes that's higher status. Often it's oddly lower: no longer in the game.

**Guilt about the team.** Especially after acquisitions where the team's fate is uncertain. The founder got out. Did the people who believed in them?

**Relief, and shame about the relief.** Many founders feel enormous relief and are ashamed of it, because relief implies they didn't love it as much as they said.

None of this gets a lot of airtime, because the founder is supposed to be happy. So it comes out sideways: as irritability, withdrawal, impulsive decisions, or a sudden fixation on something new.

## What the partner goes through

**The deferred life.** The partner has often put something on hold: a career, a move, having children, their own ambitions. The exit was when that was supposed to resume. Instead, the founder's crisis takes centre stage again.

**Disappointment that can't be voiced.** "We have money and a free spouse and I'm unhappy" sounds ungrateful. So it stays unsaid, and curdles.

**Loss of a role.** The partner's role was, in part, to be the steady one, the home base, the person who held things while the founder built. That role is also gone. Nobody notices.

**Suspicion of the restlessness.** When the founder starts talking about the next company within three months, the partner hears: *so none of that was about us.*

**Fear.** Of the founder's low mood. Of the money changing them. Of the marriage having been held together by the company's gravity and now drifting.

**Anger about the timing.** "You're falling apart now? Where was this during the Series B when I needed you?"

## The four phases after an exit

Not every couple moves through these in order, but most recognise them.

### Phase 1: Euphoria (weeks)
Celebration, relief, external validation. The marriage feels great because everyone feels great. This is not a stable state.

### Phase 2: Void (months)
The founder stops. Structure disappears. Mood drops or oscillates. The partner waits for the founder to "come back" and they don't. Conversations about "what now" go nowhere. This is where most couples get into trouble, because neither recognises what's happening as a normal transition.

### Phase 3: Reckoning (months to a year)
The money gets real. Values surface. The partner says, finally, what they deferred. The founder either starts something new (sometimes as escape) or begins to actually process the loss. This phase is where the marriage either reorganises or fractures.

### Phase 4: Reconstitution (year two onward)
A new shape. Not the old marriage plus money. A different arrangement, with different roles, possibly a different place to live, different ambitions, and a shared answer to "what is this for?" Couples who get here often describe the marriage as better than before. Couples who don't tend to separate, often amicably, often without being able to say exactly why.

## The money becomes real

During the build, the money was hypothetical. You agreed about it easily, because agreement cost nothing. Now it is in an account, and every decision about it is a decision about values you may never have compared.

How much is enough? Is the goal security, freedom, impact, more building? Does the partner get equal say over money the founder "earned"? Does the founder get to risk it on the next thing? What do you tell the children, your families, your friends who are now subtly different around you?

These are not financial questions. They are questions about who each of you is and what you want your life to mean. The money just forces them into the open. We cover this in depth in [Money and values after a liquidity event](/blog/money-and-values-after-liquidity).

## "Now it's my turn": the partner's deferred life

This deserves its own section because it is the most common fracture point we see.

The partner has an implicit contract: *I supported your thing; after the exit, you support mine.* The founder may never have heard the contract stated. Or heard it and agreed, in the abstract, years ago.

After the exit, the partner expects it to activate. The founder, in the void, has no capacity to activate it. Or, worse, launches straight into a new venture and the partner realises the contract was never going to be honoured.

If this is you: the contract needs to be said out loud, now, in words. "I put X on hold for this. I need it to be my turn, and I need to know you see that." And the founder needs to hear it as a legitimate claim, not as pressure in a moment of fragility.

The two needs, the founder's need to grieve and the partner's need to finally be prioritised, are both real and they collide. Couples who navigate this well don't rank them. They sequence them, explicitly, with a timeline both agree to.

## Who are we without the company?

The deepest question, and the one couples avoid longest.

For years, the company was the third person in the marriage, and often the main subject of it. Your conversations, your calendar, your friendships, your geography, your stress and your celebrations all routed through it. Remove it and you may discover that you don't know how to be a couple without something to build.

This isn't a sign the marriage was fake. It's a sign the marriage was organised around a project, and the project is over. The task now is to find out what you are to each other when there is nothing to optimise.

Some couples find this exhilarating. Many find it terrifying. Both reactions are reasonable.

## Self-check: Is the exit destabilising us?

Answer separately.

1. Founder: have you let yourself feel loss, or only relief and restlessness?
2. Partner: is there something you deferred that you're now waiting for the founder to notice?
3. Have you talked about what the money is *for*, beyond "security"?
4. Has the founder started, or talked about starting, something new within six months? How did the partner react?
5. Do you know what you'd talk about if the company were permanently off the table as a subject?
6. Is one of you more unhappy post-exit than during the build? Has that been said out loud?
7. Do you have a shared picture of the next three years, or two separate ones?
8. Has anyone outside the marriage, a therapist, advisor, friend, been told the truth about how this is going?

If the answers surprise you, or if you'd answer them differently from your partner, the exit is doing what exits do.

## What doesn't help

**Immediately starting the next company.** Sometimes right. Often grief avoidance, and the partner reads it as abandonment.

**A long vacation as the whole plan.** Three weeks in Italy doesn't address eighteen months of identity transition.

**Treating it as a money problem.** Hiring a wealth advisor is sensible. It doesn't touch the question of who you are to each other.

**The partner going quiet to "give space."** The founder reads it as distance, and the partner's own needs go unmet for another year.

**Assuming it will settle on its own.** Phase 2 does not resolve by waiting. It resolves by talking.

## What helps: Seven practices for the first year

### 1. Name it as a transition, not a reward
Say it out loud: "This is a loss as well as a win, and it's going to take time." Permission to grieve a success is the single most useful thing a founder can be given.

### 2. Founder: don't decide the next thing for six months
Give yourself a moratorium. Explore, read, rest, but don't commit. Decisions made in the void are made to escape it.

### 3. Partner: say the deferred thing within the first month
Don't wait for the founder to notice. "I put X on hold. I need us to make a plan for it." Early, clear, and without apology.

### 4. Sequence the two needs explicitly
"For the first three months, I'll support you through the fog. From month four, we start building toward my thing." Written down. Revisited.

### 5. Have the money conversation as a values conversation
Not "what should we do with it" but "what is it for?" Each of you answers separately first. Compare. Expect to be surprised.

### 6. Build a new shared project that isn't a company
A place, a cause, a skill, a way of living. Not to replace the company, but to give the marriage something to be *about* while you rediscover each other.

### 7. Get a third party in early
A couples therapist who understands exits can hold both of your experiences without ranking them, and can see the transition for what it is when you're both inside it.

## FAQ: Relationships after an exit

### Why do so many founders have relationship problems after selling their company?

Because the exit removes the thing that organised both partners' lives. The founder loses identity, structure and purpose, and grieves a loss nobody recognises as one. The partner, who deferred their own life waiting for "after," finds a disoriented spouse instead of a reward. Real money surfaces values disagreements that were invisible when it was hypothetical.

### Is it normal for a founder to feel depressed after an exit?

Low mood, disorientation, loss of purpose and restlessness are extremely common after an exit, even a very successful one. It mirrors what's documented in other major transitions like retirement or leaving elite sport. If low mood is persistent or severe, speak to a doctor; the transition itself is normal, but it can tip into something that needs individual help.

### How long does the adjustment after an exit take?

Most couples describe twelve to twenty-four months before a new shape settles. The hardest period is typically months two through twelve: after the celebration fades and before a new direction is clear. Couples who name the transition early and talk through it tend to move faster.

### What should the non-founder partner do after an exit?

Say the deferred thing early and clearly, rather than waiting for the founder to notice. Recognise the founder's disorientation as grief rather than ingratitude. And insist on sequencing: a defined period of supporting the founder's transition, followed by a defined shift toward the partner's own priorities.

### Should a founder start a new company right after an exit?

Not within the first six months, in most cases. The urge to start again is often a way to avoid feeling the loss of what ended, and the partner frequently experiences it as "so none of that was about us." A deliberate moratorium, followed by a decision made from a settled place, protects both the founder and the marriage.

### How does money change a marriage after an exit?

It makes previously theoretical values conflicts real: how much is enough, what the money is for, who decides, how much risk is acceptable, what to tell family and friends. These are identity questions dressed as financial ones. Couples who treat them as values conversations rather than allocation decisions fare much better.

### Can couples therapy help after an exit?

Yes, and ideally early. Therapy gives the founder room to grieve without the partner feeling abandoned again, gives the partner room to claim their turn without the founder feeling attacked, structures the money-as-values conversation, and helps you discover what you are to each other without the company as the central subject.
