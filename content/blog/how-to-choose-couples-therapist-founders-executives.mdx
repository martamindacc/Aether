You've found the therapist directory. You've filtered for "couples" and "executives." There are two hundred results, and every profile says some version of: *warm, collaborative, evidence-based, I help couples reconnect.* None of it tells you whether this person will understand what a down round does to a marriage, whether they'll let your partner finish a sentence before you reframe it, or whether they'll be so impressed by what you do that they forget to challenge you.

You run due diligence for a living. This guide gives you the questions to run it on this.

## Short answer: What should founders look for in a couples therapist?

Four things, in order.

**Baseline competence.** Formal training in an evidence-based couples method, a caseload that's substantially couples, licensed in a jurisdiction where you are, and a clear ability to hold both partners equally.

**Context fluency.** They understand what founding, leading, fundraising, exits and board dynamics do to a person and a relationship, without needing it explained, and without being dazzled by it.

**Pace and directness.** They identify what's actually happening by the end of the first or second session and give you something to work with immediately. Not six sessions of rapport-building before anything is touched.

**A format that fits your calendar.** Weekly if you can genuinely protect it; intensive or hybrid if you can't; online across time zones if you travel.

Everything else, including whether the therapist has worked in your industry, is secondary.

## Why your search is different

Standard advice on choosing a couples therapist is good as far as it goes: check credentials, ask about approach, make sure you both feel heard. For founder and executive couples, three additional problems show up that the standard advice doesn't address.

**The context problem.** When your partner says "he hasn't looked at me since the bridge round fell through," the therapist needs to know what a bridge round is and what its failure does to a founder. Not to side with the founder. To know what's load-bearing. A therapist without that fluency will treat it as a work-stress story and miss the existential weight.

**The performance problem.** You've spent years being composed under observation. You will be composed in a therapist's office. A therapist who takes your articulate, insightful account of your own pattern at face value will never reach the part of you that's actually frightened. Many therapists are relieved when a client is so cooperative. You need one who's suspicious of it.

**The format problem.** Weekly therapy assumes weeks that look alike. Yours don't. A therapist whose only offer is Tuesday at six is offering you a therapy you will fail to attend, and the failure will become evidence in your relationship.

## The baseline: what any good couples therapist must have

Before the founder-specific layer, these are non-negotiable.

**Formal couples training.** Individual therapy training does not qualify someone for couples work. Couples therapy is a distinct discipline with its own evidence base and its own failure modes. Look for completed training in a named couples model: Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), the Gottman Method, Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy, systemic/family therapy, or an established integrative couples programme. "I also see couples" is not training.

**A real couples caseload.** Ask what proportion of their current work is couples. Under a third, and couples are a sideline. You want someone who does this most of the time.

**Licensure where you are.** In the US, licensed in the state where you'll be physically located during sessions (this matters for online work across states). In Norway, authorised psychologist or recognised family therapy credential. Unlicensed "coaches" offering couples work are not bound by clinical ethics or confidentiality law.

**The ability to hold both of you.** The single biggest predictor of couples therapy failure is one partner feeling the therapist has sided with the other. You will know within two sessions. If one of you feels managed, judged or ganged up on, it won't work, regardless of credentials.

**A clear, explained approach.** They should be able to tell you how they work and why it fits your situation, in plain language, in the consultation.

## Credentials: what the letters mean and what they don't

**In the United States:**
- **LMFT** (Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist): the only US licence specifically built around couples and family work. A strong signal.
- **Psychologist (PhD/PsyD)**: doctoral-level, broad training; couples competence depends entirely on additional training.
- **LCSW / LMHC / LPC**: master's-level clinical licences; same caveat.
- **Psychiatrist (MD)**: rarely the right choice for couples work unless they have specific training.
- **Certified EFT Therapist (ICEEFT), Certified Gottman Therapist**: method-specific certifications requiring supervised practice. Strong signals of couples competence, independent of base licence.

**In Norway:**
- **Psykolog**: protected title, six-year degree plus authorisation. Couples competence still depends on further training.
- **Psykologspesialist**: specialist-level; check whether the specialisation is relevant.
- **Familieterapeut**: usually indicates postgraduate family therapy training (often a master's). A good signal for couples work.
- **Parterapeut, terapeut, coach**: not protected titles. Ask about training.

**What the letters don't tell you:** whether this person is any good with couples under high pressure, whether they'll understand your world, or whether they'll hold your partner's experience as seriously as yours. Those come from the consultation, not the profile.

## Approach: EFT, Gottman, systemic, integrative, and why it matters less than you think

Founders like to evaluate methodology. Here's the honest picture.

**Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)** works with the attachment bond between partners: the cycle you're caught in, what each of you is protecting beneath it, and reaching each other differently. Strongest research base of any couples method, with good long-term follow-up. Well suited to couples where distance, withdrawal and "we don't fight, we just don't connect" are the problem, which describes most executive couples.

**Gottman Method** is more structured and skills-oriented: assessment, specific interventions for conflict, friendship, and shared meaning. Strong research grounding in what predicts relationship success. Well suited to couples who want concrete tools and a clear framework; sometimes criticised as too mechanical for couples whose problem is emotional access.

**Systemic / family therapy** looks at patterns, roles and context, including how the business or the family of origin shapes the relationship. Useful where the company or the extended family is a strong third presence.

**Integrative** means the therapist draws on several. Most experienced couples therapists do. The question is whether they can explain *why* they'd use what with you.

The research finding that matters: differences in outcome between established couples methods are small compared to differences between therapists, and the therapeutic alliance (whether both of you trust and feel held by the therapist) predicts outcome more than method does.

So: don't choose on method. Choose on whether the therapist can explain their method, whether it's evidence-based, and whether both of you feel understood by them.

## The founder-specific layer: five things to look for

### 1. Context fluency without dazzle
They understand the vocabulary and the weight. Term sheets, vesting, board seats, earn-outs, the particular grief of a down round, the hollowness after an exit. They don't need the business explained from scratch. And, critically, they are not impressed by it. A therapist who's a little starstruck by a founder will not challenge the founder.

### 2. Pace
They move. By the end of the first or second session they've named a hypothesis about what's actually happening between you and proposed a direction. They don't spend six sessions "building the alliance" before touching anything. High performers disengage from process that doesn't go anywhere, and a good therapist for this population knows it.

### 3. Experience with the performance problem
They've worked with people who are composed under observation, and they know what to do about it: not confront it, not accept it, but work around it with duration, specificity and patient refusal to take the polished answer. Ask them about it directly (question 7 below). A therapist who hasn't thought about this will give a thin answer.

### 4. Structural awareness of asymmetry
They see, without being told, that one partner's consuming career and the other's invisible load is a structure, not a complaint. They don't treat the non-founder partner as the supportive backdrop. [See: When your partner is the founder.](/blog/when-your-partner-is-the-founder)

### 5. Format flexibility
They offer more than weekly. Intensives, online across time zones, sessions from wherever you are, scheduling around professional events. A therapist with one format is offering you a therapy built for someone else's life.

## Fluency vs. flattery: the context trap

The most common mistake founder couples make in choosing a therapist is confusing *understands my world* with *is impressed by my world.*

They look alike in a consultation. The therapist nods knowingly at "Series B," asks interested questions about the company, maybe mentions other founders they've worked with. It feels like being understood.

What it may actually be is a therapist who is going to defer to the founder, treat the founder's stress as the main event, and quietly cast the partner as the one who needs to be more understanding. The founder leaves consultations like this feeling great. The partner leaves feeling like they've just hired someone for the other side.

The test: in the consultation, does the therapist show as much curiosity about the *non-founder* partner's experience as about the business? Do they ask the partner what the last three years have cost them? If all the interested questions go toward the company, that's flattery, not fluency.

## Format: weekly, intensive, online, hybrid

A brief map; [the full decision framework is in Intensive vs weekly](/blog/intensive-vs-weekly-couples-therapy).

**Weekly** works if you can genuinely protect the same hour for three to six months. Be honest about your history, not your intentions. If you've cancelled a third of anything recurring in the last year, you won't protect this either.

**Intensive** (one to three concentrated days) works when the calendar defeats weekly, when something urgent has happened, or when you've stalled in weekly. It also addresses the performance problem through duration. [See: Couples intensives for executives and founders.](/blog/couples-intensive-executives-founders)

**Online** is essential if either of you travels, and it should include two-location sessions (each of you from a different place) as a standard option, not a workaround.

**Hybrid** (intensive to break through, periodic sessions to consolidate, annual booster before a known pressure point) is what most founder couples who stay in therapy end up doing.

Ask the therapist which formats they offer, and whether they'd recommend a different one for you. A therapist who only offers one format will recommend that format.

## Discretion: beyond "everything is confidential"

All licensed therapists are bound by confidentiality, with legal limits. For public-facing founders and executives, the standard line isn't enough. Ask about:

**Physical discretion.** Where is the office? Is it a building you'd be seen entering? Do they offer online as a default rather than an exception?

**Record-keeping.** What goes in the clinical record? If you pay privately and don't submit for insurance, there's no diagnosis in any external system. If you do submit, one partner typically needs a diagnosis code on the superbill. Know this before you decide.

**Marketing.** Do they use client stories, testimonials or case examples? How anonymised? Some practices publish composite cases; make sure yours couldn't be recognised.

**Dual relationships.** In a small ecosystem (a city's startup scene, an industry), does the therapist see other founders you know? Investors? Your co-founder? They can't tell you who, but they can tell you whether they screen for it and what they'd do if a conflict emerged.

**Data.** What platform for video? Where are notes stored? How long are they kept?

A therapist who takes discretion seriously will have thought about all of this and will answer easily. One who hasn't will be vague.

## The twelve consultation questions

Most practices offer a short free consultation. Use it. Ask these, and listen as much to how they answer as to what they say.

**Baseline**
1. What proportion of your current caseload is couples?
2. What formal couples training have you completed, and in which model?
3. How do you make sure both partners feel equally held, especially when one is more forthcoming than the other?

**Context**
4. What's your experience with founders, executives or couples where one partner's career dominates the household?
5. Can you give an example, anonymised, of how the business context has shown up in your couples work?

**Pace and style**
6. How quickly do you typically identify what's actually going on between a couple, and what does the first session produce?
7. Some people are very composed and articulate in therapy, and nothing moves. How do you work with that?

**Format**
8. Which formats do you offer: weekly, intensive, online, two-location? Which would you recommend for us, and why?
9. How do you handle a client who travels constantly or lives between cities?

**Discretion**
10. Beyond standard confidentiality, how do you handle privacy for clients in public-facing roles?
11. Do you screen for dual relationships in a small professional ecosystem?

**Fit**
12. Based on what we've told you, what's your initial read, and would you recommend us to someone else if you're not the right fit?

Question 12 is the most revealing. A therapist who would never refer out is selling.

## Eight red flags

1. **They take the founder's side, subtly.** More questions about the business than about the partner. Reframing the partner's complaints as "understandable stress." You'll feel it in the consultation.

2. **They're impressed.** Interest in your company, your round, your exit, beyond what's clinically relevant. Flattery is not fluency.

3. **They can't name their method or explain why it fits you.** "I'm eclectic" with nothing behind it.

4. **Couples are a sideline.** Under a third of their caseload, or vague about the number.

5. **One format only.** Tuesday at six or nothing.

6. **They accept the polished answer.** In the consultation, if you deliver an insightful, articulate account of your pattern and they simply agree, they'll keep agreeing. You need someone who hears the polish as data.

7. **They promise outcomes.** "I can save your marriage." No ethical therapist promises results.

8. **They can't tell you what they'd do if they weren't the right fit.** Question 12 again. A therapist with no referral network and no willingness to use it is a therapist who'll keep you regardless of fit.

## The reluctant partner and the consultation

In founder and executive couples, one partner is usually more reluctant, and it's often the founder.

If you're the willing partner, don't choose alone and present a decision. Shortlist two or three, share the shortlist, and let the reluctant partner weigh in or even choose. A therapist the reluctant partner picked is a therapist they can't dismiss as "yours."

Use the free consultation as the low-commitment first step. Fifteen minutes is easier to say yes to than "therapy." And watch the reluctant partner during it: a good therapist will make them feel safe, not managed. If they come out of the consultation more willing, that's your answer. If they come out more closed, keep looking.


## Running the search: a process that doesn't become a project

Founders can turn anything into a project. Here's a process bounded enough not to become one.

**Week 1: Shortlist of three.** Sources: directories filtered by couples specialisation and your area (Psychology Today, Zencare, ICEEFT and Gottman referral networks in the US; Norsk Psykologforening in Norway); referrals from a trusted friend who's done this; the practices whose content actually answered your questions. Three, not ten.

**Week 1–2: Three consultations.** Both of you present at each. Fifteen to twenty minutes. The twelve questions, or the ones that matter most to you. Take two minutes afterward, separately, to write down your gut read before discussing.

**Week 2: Decide.** Compare notes. If you disagree, the reluctant partner's preference carries more weight, for the reason above. If none of the three fits, ask the one who came closest for a referral; good therapists know who's good.

**Week 2–3: First session.** See below for how to judge it.

**Hard stop:** if the search is still running at week four, you're procrastinating with process. Pick the best of what you've seen and start. Switching later is allowed.

## What to expect from the first session, and how to judge it

A good first couples session, in any format, does three things: hears both partners' accounts without rushing either, begins to identify the pattern between you, and gives you a sense of direction.

Judge it on:

- **Did you both get airtime?** Roughly equal, and did the therapist protect the quieter partner's time?
- **Did the therapist see something you hadn't?** Not a verdict. An observation about how you interact that landed as true.
- **Did the founder's composure get past the therapist?** If you performed and they bought it, that's a problem.
- **Did the partner feel the business was treated as context, not as the main character?**
- **Did you leave with something?** A name for the pattern, a thing to notice this week, a direction. Not a plan to build rapport for six more sessions.
- **Did you both want to go back?** Not enjoy it. Want to go back.

If three or more of these are no, it's probably the wrong fit. Give it one more session if you're unsure, then decide.

## When to switch therapists

Founders tend to either stick with a bad fit too long (sunk cost, not wanting to "fail" at therapy) or switch too fast (the therapist challenged them and it was uncomfortable). The honest signals:

**Switch if:** one of you consistently feels sided-against after four sessions; the therapist has accepted the polished version and nothing has moved in two months; the format is being failed and the therapist won't adapt it; a dual-relationship conflict emerges; or the therapist is clearly more interested in the business than in the two of you.

**Don't switch if:** the discomfort is the work (the therapist is reaching something you'd rather not feel); you disagree with one observation (that's material, not malpractice); or the reluctant partner wants to switch because they were challenged.

Tell the therapist before you leave. A good one will help you find the next one.

## Cost, insurance and the Good Faith Estimate

Briefly, because [the full guide is here](/guides/couples-therapy-cost-insurance-nyc-california):

Couples therapy with a specialist in New York or California typically runs US$200–450 per session, higher for intensive formats and for highly experienced clinicians. Most couples specialists are private-pay. US insurers reimburse couples therapy only as "family psychotherapy with patient present" (CPT 90847) when one partner has a diagnosis, which has implications for your records. Out-of-network reimbursement, where your plan offers it, returns 50–80% of an "allowed amount" after the OON deductible. HSA/FSA funds can usually be used. Any private-pay provider must give you a written Good Faith Estimate before care begins.

For most founder and executive couples, cost is not the constraint. Fit is. Don't let a slightly lower rate decide this.

## FAQ: Choosing a couples therapist as a founder or executive

### Does my couples therapist need to have experience with founders?

They need context fluency: an understanding of what fundraising, exits, board dynamics and consuming leadership roles do to a person and a relationship, so you don't have to explain your world from scratch and they don't miss what's load-bearing. They don't need to have worked in your industry, and they must not be impressed by it. Fluency without flattery is the test.

### What's the most important quality in a couples therapist?

The ability to hold both partners equally. Across all couples therapy research, one partner feeling the therapist has sided with the other is the strongest predictor of failure. For founder couples specifically, the risk is a therapist who treats the founder's stress as the main event and the partner as supporting cast. You'll feel it within two sessions.

### Should we choose EFT, Gottman or something else?

Method matters less than therapist. Differences in outcome between established evidence-based couples methods are small compared to differences between individual therapists, and the alliance predicts outcome more than approach. Choose a therapist who can explain their method, whose method is evidence-based, and who both of you feel understood by. EFT suits distance-and-withdrawal couples well; Gottman suits couples who want structured tools.

### What should I ask a couples therapist in a consultation?

The proportion of their caseload that's couples, their formal couples training, how they hold both partners equally, their experience with couples where one career dominates, how fast they identify the pattern, how they work with very composed clients, which formats they offer, how they handle privacy for public-facing clients, and whether they'd refer you elsewhere if they weren't the right fit. That last one is the most revealing.

### How do I know if a therapist is taking my partner's side?

You'll feel managed rather than heard. The therapist asks more about the other partner's world than yours, reframes your concerns as understandable reactions to the other's stress, and the sessions leave you feeling like you've hired someone for the opposing side. If this persists past the second session, say so; if it doesn't change, switch.

### My partner is a founder and keeps cancelling. What format should we choose?

Not weekly. Choose an intensive (one to three concentrated days) or a hybrid, with online two-location sessions for when one of you travels. A single weekend that actually happens beats fifteen Tuesdays that get moved, and the format itself removes the cancellation pattern that otherwise becomes evidence in the relationship.

### How much does a couples therapist for executives cost?

In New York and California, specialist couples therapy typically runs US$200–450 per session, with intensives priced as packages (roughly US$2,500–7,500 for a weekend). Most are private-pay with superbills for out-of-network reimbursement where your plan allows. For most executive couples the real constraint is fit and format, not fee.
