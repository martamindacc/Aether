You moved here for a job, for love, for a change. Oslo is beautiful, orderly, expensive, and quieter than you expected. And somewhere between the relocation, the dark winters, the new jobs and the distance from everyone you know, your relationship started to strain.

Maybe you argue more. Maybe you've stopped arguing and just coexist. Maybe one of you is thriving here and the other is quietly drowning. And when you look for help, you hit a wall: websites in Norwegian, a public system you don't understand, and the suspicion that therapy in your second (or third) language won't reach the things that actually matter.

Here's the reassuring part: English-speaking couples therapy in Oslo exists, it's accessible, and the process is simpler than the Norwegian websites make it look.

## Short answer: Can we get couples therapy in English in Oslo?

Yes. Several private practices in Oslo offer couples therapy in English, in person and online. You can book directly, without a referral from a GP (fastlege), without a Norwegian national ID number, and without being enrolled in the public health system. Sessions typically cost 1,700–4,000 NOK per hour, and most practices offer evening or online appointments.

The public alternative, familievernkontoret, is free but primarily Norwegian-speaking, has waiting times of several weeks, and prioritises families with children under 18. For most international couples, private therapy is the realistic route.

## How the Norwegian system works, and why it confuses internationals

Norway has excellent public healthcare, which leads many internationals to assume couples therapy must be part of it. It mostly isn't.

**Psychologists (psykolog)** in the public system treat diagnosed mental health conditions, usually via referral from your fastlege. Couples therapy is not a diagnosis, so it falls outside this system. Public psychologists in private practice with public funding (avtalespesialister) have long waiting lists and rarely take couples.

**Familievernkontoret** is the state family counselling service, run by Bufetat. It offers couples therapy, family therapy, and mandatory mediation for separating parents. It's free and needs no referral. But it operates in Norwegian (some offices can arrange interpreters, and some therapists speak English, but it isn't designed for it), aims for a four-week wait that often stretches longer, and prioritises families with children at home. Couples without children may wait a long time or receive a shorter course.

**Private practitioners** are where English-speaking couples therapy actually happens. Psychologists, family therapists and certified couples therapists in private practice, who set their own fees, choose their languages, and can see you within days.

In short: In Norway, couples therapy is mostly a private service. That's true for Norwegians too; it's just that Norwegians know it.

## Why international couples face different pressures

Relationship difficulties are universal. But international couples in Norway often carry additional loads that locals don't, and that standard relationship advice ignores:

**Relocation asymmetry.** One of you moved for a job; the other followed. The one who followed often loses career, network and identity in the move, and that loss rarely gets named until it becomes resentment.

**No support network.** No parents to take the kids for a weekend. No old friends who know you both. The relationship has to carry everything, and it wasn't built for that.

**Cultural friction with Norway itself.** Norwegian workplace culture, social reserve, the darkness, the cost of living, the particular way Norwegian friendships form slowly. One of you may adapt faster than the other, and the gap becomes a relationship problem.

**Language as a wedge.** If one of you is learning Norwegian faster, or using it at work, you start living in slightly different worlds. And if you're from different countries, you may already be working in a shared second language, with all the nuance that gets lost.

**Visa and residency dependency.** When one partner's residence permit depends on the other's job or on the relationship itself, there's a power imbalance in the room that nobody wants to acknowledge.

**Distance from family at the wrong moments.** Illness back home, ageing parents, missed weddings and funerals. Grief and guilt that the partner who is "at home" here can't fully share.

**"Should we stay or go back?"** The question that sits under everything. Often one of you wants to stay and the other doesn't, and it gets argued about through other topics.

A therapist who understands these dynamics will hear them underneath the arguments about chores and money. One who doesn't will treat you like any other couple, and miss what's load-bearing.

## Mixed couples: When one of you is Norwegian

When one partner is Norwegian and the other isn't, the dynamics shift again:

- **Home advantage.** The Norwegian partner has family, friends, language and cultural fluency. The international partner has you. That imbalance is real even in the most loving relationship.
- **Different norms around conflict and emotion.** Norwegian communication tends toward understatement and avoidance of open conflict. If you're from a more expressive culture, you may read your partner's calm as coldness, and they may read your intensity as aggression.
- **Family expectations.** Christmas where? Whose traditions? How involved should in-laws be? These questions carry more weight when the cultures differ.
- **Language at home.** Which language do you argue in? Which do you comfort each other in? Does one of you always have the linguistic upper hand?
- **The Norwegian partner's guilt.** Often the Norwegian partner feels responsible for the other's unhappiness here, and that guilt turns into defensiveness.

Therapy in English puts both of you on equal linguistic footing, which matters more than most mixed couples expect. And a therapist who can hold both cultural frames, rather than defaulting to Norwegian norms as "normal," is worth looking for specifically.

## Signs you might benefit from couples therapy

You don't need to be in crisis. Consider therapy if:

- You have the same argument repeatedly without resolution
- One of you has withdrawn emotionally; you live side by side rather than together
- The move to Norway has become a recurring source of conflict or blame
- Intimacy has faded and you can't talk about it
- One of you is thriving here and the other isn't, and it's creating distance
- You're wondering whether to stay together, or whether to stay in Norway
- Trust has been broken
- You function well as a logistics team but have stopped being partners

## How to choose an English-speaking couples therapist in Oslo

**Check that couples work is a specialisation, not a sideline.** Many therapists "also see couples." You want someone for whom couples are a core part of the practice. Ask directly: What proportion of your work is with couples?

**Ask about their English.** Not "do you speak English" but: Do you conduct therapy in English regularly? Can you do emotional nuance in English, or just logistics? Many Norwegians have excellent English for work and thinner English for feelings. You need the latter.

**Ask about experience with international couples.** Have they worked with expats? With mixed Norwegian-international couples? Do they understand relocation dynamics?

**Ask about approach.** Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), Gottman Method, systemic therapy and integrative approaches are all evidence-based. What matters is that they can explain why their approach fits you.

**Check practicalities.** Evening hours? Online sessions when one of you travels? How soon can you start?

**Use the free consultation.** Most practices offer a short introductory call. Use it to check whether you both feel understood. If one of you doesn't, keep looking. A therapist who favours one partner, even subtly, won't work.

**Credentials in Norway.** "Psykolog" is a protected title requiring a six-year degree and authorisation. "Familieterapeut" usually indicates postgraduate family therapy training. "Parterapeut" and "terapeut" are not protected titles, so ask about training. None of this guarantees quality, but it helps you ask the right questions.

## What to expect in your first session

The first session is usually 60–90 minutes. The therapist will ask:

- How you met and what the relationship was like at its best
- What has changed, and when
- What each of you sees as the main problem (you don't have to agree)
- What brought you to Norway, and how that's gone for each of you
- What you each hope therapy can do

You don't need to prepare anything. You don't need to agree on what the problem is. A good therapist expects different perspectives and won't take sides.

By the end of the first session, you should have a sense of whether the therapist understands you both, and a rough sense of a way forward. If you don't, that's useful information too.

## Does insurance cover it?

**Norwegian public health (HELFO):** No. Couples therapy isn't a reimbursable health service.

**Norwegian private health insurance (e.g. through your employer):** Sometimes, for sessions with authorised psychologists specifically. Check whether "psykolog" sessions are covered and whether couples sessions qualify. Many plans cover individual therapy but not couples.

**International health insurance (Cigna Global, Allianz, Bupa, etc.):** Often yes, under mental health or outpatient benefits, with varying limits. Check whether the practitioner's credentials meet their criteria, and whether you need pre-authorisation.

**Employer wellbeing budgets:** Increasingly common at international companies in Oslo. Some will reimburse therapy without going through insurance at all. Worth asking HR, discreetly.

Private practices will give you an invoice (faktura) you can submit yourself.

## Online couples therapy: An option many internationals prefer

Online couples therapy is well established and well researched, and for international couples it has specific advantages:

- **One of you travels.** Sessions continue from a hotel room.
- **You live outside Oslo.** Stavanger, Bergen, Trondheim, Tromsø: you can still work with an Oslo-based English-speaking therapist.
- **Discretion.** No waiting rooms. Particularly valued in Oslo's small international community, where you may know people at the same clinic.
- **You're not sure you're staying.** Online therapy continues if you relocate.
- **Winter.** Enough said.

Research indicates that video-based couples therapy achieves outcomes comparable to in-person work. The main requirement is a private space where you can both speak freely.

## FAQ: Couples therapy in Oslo in English

### Can I get couples therapy in English in Oslo?

Yes. Several private practices in Oslo offer couples therapy in English, in person and online. You book directly, without a referral or Norwegian ID number. Sessions typically cost 1,700–4,000 NOK per hour.

### Do I need a referral from my fastlege for couples therapy?

No. Couples therapy in Norway is a private service for which you book directly. Your fastlege can't refer you for couples therapy through the public system, as it isn't a medical treatment.

### Is couples therapy free in Norway?

The public family counselling service (familievernkontoret) offers free couples therapy, but it operates primarily in Norwegian, has waiting times of several weeks, and prioritises families with children under 18. Private couples therapy, where English is widely available, is paid.

### How much does couples therapy cost in Oslo?

Typically 1,700–4,000 NOK per session, depending on the therapist and session length. A full course of 10–15 sessions usually totals 17,000–40,000 NOK. At Aether Practice, a 60-minute session is 1,700 NOK.

### Does Norwegian health insurance cover couples therapy?

Public insurance (HELFO) does not. Some employer private insurance plans cover sessions with authorised psychologists; check whether couples sessions qualify. International insurance plans often cover it under mental health benefits.

### We're a mixed couple, one Norwegian and one not. Is therapy in English still right?

Often yes, because it puts you on equal linguistic footing. Many Norwegians have excellent English for work and thinner English for emotion, so check that your partner is comfortable. Some couples alternate or use both; a bilingual therapist can accommodate that.

### Can we do couples therapy online from outside Oslo?

Yes. Online couples therapy is well researched and widely offered. It suits couples who live elsewhere in Norway, travel frequently, or prefer discretion. Aether Practice works with couples online across Norway.

### What if only one of us wants therapy?

Very common, especially when one partner is struggling with life in Norway and the other isn't. You can start alone: individual sessions focused on the relationship often shift the dynamic enough that the other partner joins later.
