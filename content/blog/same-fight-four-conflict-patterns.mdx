It starts with something small. A comment about the dishwasher not being emptied. A sigh. A tone.

Twenty minutes later you're standing in the living room arguing about who does more, who never listens, and what happened at Easter three years ago. Neither of you quite remembers how you got here. But you both know exactly where it ends: one of you leaves the room, the other stays standing there, and tomorrow you pretend nothing happened.

You've had this fight before. Maybe a hundred times. And you'll have it again, unless you understand what it actually is.

## Short answer: Why do we keep having the same fight?

Because you aren't fighting about the topic. You're fighting in a pattern.

A conflict pattern is a fixed sequence of reactions that fires regardless of what the argument is about. One of you does A, the other answers with B, which makes the first do more A. The pattern runs on autopilot, and the topic is only the fuel. That's why it feels like you never get anywhere: you solve "the dishwasher", but the pattern is still there next time.

When you can see the pattern, you can stop it. When you only see the topic, you can't.

## The topic isn't the problem. The pattern is.

Research on couples shows something surprising: a large share of the conflicts couples have are about recurring issues that never get "solved", because they rest on fundamental differences in personality, needs or values. This is true of happy couples too.

The difference between couples who do well and couples who struggle is not whether they have recurring conflicts. It is *how* they have them, and whether they manage to repair afterwards.

So if you fight about the same thing again and again, the solution is not necessarily to agree about the topic. The solution is to change how you fight.

## The four conflict patterns

### 1. Pursue–withdraw

The most common pattern in relationships, and the one that most often ends up in therapy.

**What it looks like:** One partner raises a problem, often with frustration. The other goes quiet, minimises, or leaves. The first feels unheard and turns up the intensity. The second feels attacked and withdraws further. The more one pursues, the more the other withdraws. The more the other withdraws, the more the first pursues.

**What gets said:**
"You never listen to me!"
"I can't deal with this right now."

**What is meant:**
"I'm afraid you don't care about me."
"I'm afraid I never get it right."

**The danger:** Over time, the pursuer gives up. It looks like peace, but it's distance.

### 2. Attack–attack (escalation)

**What it looks like:** Both defend by attacking. Criticism is met with counter-criticism. Old grievances come out. Voices rise. Nobody backs down, so the fight escalates until someone says something they regret.

**What gets said:**
"You never do anything around here!"
"Oh really? And you haven't been at work until nine every night this week?"

**What is meant:**
"I feel alone with the responsibility."
"I feel treated unfairly no matter what I do."

**The danger:** Contempt. When criticism shifts from "you did something stupid" to "you are stupid", the relationship is in real danger. Contempt is the single factor researchers link most strongly to separation. [Read more in The Four Horsemen and their antidotes.](/blog/four-horsemen-gottman-antidotes)

### 3. Withdraw–withdraw (mutual avoidance)

**What it looks like:** Nobody raises anything. You've learned that conflict leads nowhere, so you stop. The surface is calm. Underneath lies everything you haven't said.

**What gets said:**
"It's fine."
"Let's talk about it another time."

**What is meant:**
"I've given up on being understood."
"It's safer to be alone in this than to try again."

**The danger:** This pattern doesn't look like fighting, so couples often discover it late. But it's the pattern that most often leads to one partner saying "I'm done" one day, seemingly out of nowhere.

### 4. Criticise–defend (accuse–explain)

**What it looks like:** One partner criticises. The other responds not with a counter-attack or withdrawal but with explanation, excuse or justification. The first feels responsibility is never taken. The second feels nothing is ever good enough.

**What gets said:**
"You forgot to call the daycare again."
"Yes, but I had three meetings back to back, and you could have done it too."

**What is meant:**
"I need to be able to rely on you."
"I need you to see how hard I'm trying."

**The danger:** The one who criticises becomes more precise and harder in the criticism, because it feels like the only way to be heard. The one who defends becomes more and more sensitive. The pattern often slides into pursue–withdraw or attack–attack.

| Pattern | One partner | The other partner | Hallmark | Underlying fear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pursue–withdraw** | Raises, presses, criticises | Goes quiet, leaves, minimises | One talks, one is silent | Being abandoned / not measuring up |
| **Attack–attack** | Attacks | Attacks back | Escalation, high temperature | Being dominated / treated unfairly |
| **Withdraw–withdraw** | Avoids | Avoids | Quiet, "fine", distance | Not being understood no matter what |
| **Criticise–defend** | Accuses | Explains, justifies | "Yes, but …" | Not being able to rely on / not being good enough |

## Self-test: Which pattern is yours?

Think about the last fight you had. Answer separately, then compare.

**When we disagree, I tend to:**
a) Raise it directly, often with frustration, and keep going until I get an answer
b) Answer sharply, and bring up things from before
c) Go quiet, withdraw or change the subject
d) Explain why I did what I did

**When my partner is frustrated, I tend to:**
a) Ask what's wrong and not let up until we've talked it through
b) Get irritated and say what I'm unhappy about
c) Wait for it to pass
d) Defend myself and explain my side

**After a fight we usually:**
a) One wants to finish talking, the other wants to be left alone
b) Both stay angry for a long time
c) Pretend nothing happened
d) One feels the other never took responsibility

**Result:**
- Mostly **a** for one and **c** for the other: **Pursue–withdraw.**
- Mostly **b** for both: **Attack–attack.**
- Mostly **c** for both: **Withdraw–withdraw.**
- Mostly **a** for one and **d** for the other: **Criticise–defend.**

Most couples have one dominant pattern and one secondary. Pursue–withdraw that escalates into attack–attack is common. Attack–attack that burns out into withdraw–withdraw is also common.

## What's really happening under the pattern

In Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) the conflict pattern is described as a "negative cycle", and the most important point is this: **the pattern is the enemy, not your partner.**

Under every pattern lie two sets of feelings:

**Surface feelings** are what you show: anger, irritation, coldness, indifference.

**Underlying feelings** are what you protect: fear of being left, fear of not being good enough, fear of being controlled, shame at not measuring up.

Take the pursue–withdraw pattern: the pursuer shows anger but feels fear of losing contact. The withdrawer shows indifference but feels that they never get it right. If both could say the second thing instead of showing the first, the fight would look completely different.

That's what couples therapy works on: making it safe enough to say what lies underneath.

## How to break the pattern: Five concrete moves

### 1. Give the pattern a name
When you can say "there we are in the cycle again", you've already taken a step out of it. Some couples call it "the loop", "the dance" or something more personal. The point is that the pattern becomes a third party you can talk about, not something one of you does to the other.

### 2. Learn your own early warnings
What happens in your body right before you attack, defend or withdraw? Heat in the face, tight shoulders, a particular thought ("here it comes again")? That's where you can stop. Not in the middle of the fight, but at the first warning.

### 3. Use a pause with a return
Leaving the room isn't wrong. Leaving without saying when you'll be back is. "I need twenty minutes, then we'll continue" breaks the pursue–withdraw pattern because the pursuer knows the contact isn't gone. And: actually come back.

### 4. Say the need, not the accusation
"You never listen to me" is an accusation that triggers defence. "I need to know that what I say matters to you" is a need that invites contact. Same feeling. Completely different effect.

### 5. Ask about what's underneath
When your partner attacks, ask: "What are you actually afraid of right now?" When your partner withdraws, ask: "What happens inside you when I bring this up?" It feels artificial at first. It works anyway.

## Repair: The skill that separates couples who stay together

Research on what distinguishes stable couples from couples who separate doesn't point first to how much they fight. It points to whether they manage to *repair*.

Repair is everything you do to break escalation and restore contact, during or after a conflict: a hand on the arm, a joke, "sorry, that came out wrong", "can we start over?".

Couples who have the same fight over and over are usually not short of conflict resolution. They're short of repair. Every unresolved fight settles on top of the last one, until the pattern has become a mountain.

Start with this: after your next fight, regardless of who "was right", one of you says: "That didn't go well. I want us to be okay again." That's all. You don't need to solve the topic. You just need to repair the contact.

## When you can't break it alone

Some patterns are too entrenched to break at home. That isn't a failure. It's how patterns work: they run on autopilot, and autopilot is hard to see from the inside.

Couples therapy gives you a third person who sees the pattern from outside, stops it in real time, and helps you say what lies underneath. For most couples, this is what finally makes "the same fight" stop repeating itself.

## FAQ: Fighting in relationships

### Why do we keep having the same fight?

Because you're fighting in a pattern, not about a topic. The pattern is a fixed sequence of reactions, for example criticism, defence, escalation and withdrawal, that fires regardless of what the fight is about. When the pattern doesn't change, the fight repeats even when the topic gets "solved".

### Is it normal to fight in a relationship?

Yes. All couples have conflict, and many conflicts recur because they rest on fundamental differences. What matters is not whether you fight, but how you fight, and whether you manage to repair afterwards.

### How much fighting is too much?

There's no numerical limit. The warning signs are that fights escalate quickly, contain contempt or condescension, are never repaired, or that you've stopped fighting because it's pointless. If you recognise one or more of these, it's worth addressing.

### What is the most dangerous conflict pattern?

Contempt, meaning condescension, mockery and "you're hopeless" attitudes, is the single element researchers link most strongly to separation. Among the patterns, withdraw–withdraw is often the most dangerous in the long run, because it looks like peace while the distance grows.

### How do you stop fighting about small things?

The small things are rarely the problem. Find out which pattern fires, what each of you is really afraid of, and practise saying the need instead of the accusation. And practise repair afterwards. That reduces fights about small things more than any agreement about the dishwasher.

### Can couples therapy help with fighting?

Yes. Breaking entrenched conflict patterns is one of the things couples therapy is best documented for. The therapist helps you see the pattern from outside, stop it in real time and bring out what lies underneath, so you can meet each other instead of fighting.

### What if only one of us wants to change the pattern?

A pattern takes two to run, so when one of you changes, the pattern changes. But it's hard to sustain alone over time. You can start with individual sessions focused on the relationship; many reluctant partners join later when they see something is actually shifting.
