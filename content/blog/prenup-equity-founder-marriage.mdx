It came up over dinner, or via a lawyer, or because an investor asked. Someone said the word "prenup," or "postnup," or "we should think about what happens to the shares if." And the temperature in the room changed.

One of you heard: *I'm being asked to plan for our divorce.* The other heard: *I'm being asked to risk everything I've built on a relationship, and asking for protection makes me the villain.* Neither of you said either of those things. You talked about lawyers and timelines and "being sensible." And something between you got a little colder.

This is the conversation founder couples most need to have well and most often have badly, because everyone around them treats it as a legal matter when it is, first, a relational one.

## Short answer: Why is this conversation so hard for founder couples?

Because a prenup or equity agreement forces two things into the open that most couples prefer to leave implicit: *what is yours versus ours*, and *what happens if this ends*.

For a founder, the company may be the most significant thing they have ever made. It is identity, livelihood, obligation to others, and years of work, often predating the relationship. The impulse to protect it is not a lack of love.

For the partner, being asked to sign something that carves the company out of the marriage can feel like being told: the most important thing in your life is not something we share. That's not paranoia either.

Both of these are legitimate, and the conversation fails when either partner treats the other's position as the unreasonable one.

## Who's asking for the prenup, and why that matters

The dynamics change depending on who raises it.

**The founder raises it.** Often prompted by lawyers, investors or a co-founder. The partner may hear: you don't trust me, or you're preparing for this to fail. The founder may feel: I'm being responsible, and I'm being punished for it.

**The partner raises it.** Less common, but it happens, especially when the partner has their own assets or has seen what founder divorces do. The founder may feel suspected, or relieved.

**A third party raises it.** Investors, the board, co-founders, a parent. Now the couple is being asked to resolve, between themselves, a demand that came from outside. The risk is that the partner becomes the obstacle to a business requirement, and the founder becomes the messenger for people who don't care about the marriage.

**Nobody raises it, and it sits there.** Both know it should be discussed. Neither wants to be the one. The silence itself becomes a presence.

Knowing who raised it and why tells you what the other partner is likely hearing.

## What each partner is actually afraid of

Underneath the legal conversation are predictable fears. Naming them is most of the work.

**The founder may fear:**
- Losing control of the company in a divorce, and with it the team, the mission, years of work
- Dilution or forced sale that harms co-founders and investors who trusted them
- Being seen as mercenary for wanting protection
- That raising it will damage the relationship they're trying to protect
- That not raising it will be seen as naive by everyone whose opinion they value professionally

**The partner may fear:**
- Being defined as outside the most important thing in the founder's life
- Years of contribution, support, deferred career, invisible labour, counting for nothing
- Being left with nothing if the relationship ends, after having absorbed all the risk
- That the request signals the founder is already planning an exit from the marriage
- Being painted as greedy for wanting their contribution recognised

Most prenup fights are two of these fears colliding without either being spoken.

## "Protecting the company" vs "protecting myself from you"

This is the distinction that unlocks most of these conversations.

There is a legitimate business interest in making sure a divorce doesn't destabilise a company: that shares can't be forced into the hands of a non-operating ex-spouse, that the cap table isn't litigated, that co-founders and investors aren't harmed by the founder's personal life. Many partners, when it's explained this way, understand it completely. They don't want to be in a position to damage a company either.

There is a separate question: what does the partner receive, fairly, in recognition of their contribution to the life that made the company possible, if the marriage ends?

Founders often conflate these. They ask for the first and, by not addressing the second, communicate: *I want the company protected and I haven't thought about you.* Partners hear the second even when only the first was meant.

Couples who do this well separate them explicitly. "I need the company protected from a divorce, for the sake of everyone in it. And separately, I want to make sure that if this ever ended, you'd be treated fairly for everything you've put in. Let's design both." That sentence, or something like it, changes the entire conversation.

## The equity question: whose is it?

The founder's name is on the shares. The founder did the visible work. In many jurisdictions, equity acquired before marriage is separate property, equity acquired during may not be, and appreciation is complicated. This is a legal question with a legal answer that varies by place; get a lawyer.

The relational question is different: *what does each of you believe?*

A founder who believes the company is entirely theirs, because they built it, is not wrong about the building. A partner who believes the company is partly theirs, because they held the life that made the building possible, is not wrong about the holding. If these beliefs are never spoken, the legal documents will be read by each partner through their own unspoken belief, and the other's position will feel like theft.

Say the beliefs out loud before the lawyers draft anything. The documents should reflect a shared understanding, not impose one.

## Timing: before the wedding, after the raise, or when the money is real

**Before the wedding.** Cleanest legally, hardest emotionally. The relationship is at its most romantic, and the conversation is at its least. The advantage: no company value to fight over yet, so it's about principles. The risk: it feels like a vote of no confidence at the moment of commitment.

**After a raise or when the company has real value.** The most common trigger, and the most fraught. Now there is something concrete to protect, and the partner may reasonably ask why it wasn't discussed before there was something to lose.

**At liquidity.** When the money is real, the conversation is usually about postnups and estate planning rather than prenups, and it merges with the broader values conversation. [Read: Money and values after a liquidity event.](/blog/money-and-values-after-liquidity)

The relational principle: earlier is better, and whenever you have it, acknowledge the timing. "I know this is coming late, and I know it's coming because the company got real. I should have raised it before." Owning the awkwardness disarms it.

## When investors or co-founders push

Investors sometimes want a founder's spouse to sign a consent or waiver so that a divorce can't disrupt the company. Co-founders may want the same. This is a reasonable business request. It is also a moment where the founder has to decide whose side of the table they're sitting on.

The mistake: the founder relays the request to the partner as a business requirement. "The investors need you to sign this." The partner becomes an obstacle to be managed. The founder becomes a messenger for strangers.

The better approach: the founder sits on the partner's side first. "The investors want this. I understand why. Before we deal with them, I want us to agree what's fair between us, and then we'll handle their request together." The partner is a co-decider, not a signatory.

If the founder can't do this, it tells the partner something about where they rank, and no document will fix what that communicates.

## Postnups and the already-married founder

Many founder couples married before the company existed, or before it was worth anything. Now there is value, and the conversation arrives mid-marriage, usually triggered by a lawyer, a raise, or an estate plan.

The postnup conversation is often harder than the prenup, because the partner has already invested years without any agreement, and is now being asked to formalise something after the fact. The partner may feel the terms are being changed mid-game.

What helps: frame it as *clarifying* rather than *limiting*. "We never wrote down what we both believe about this, and now it matters. I want us to agree, in writing, on what's fair, including what's fair to you." A postnup that recognises the partner's contribution can strengthen a marriage. One that only protects the company can quietly end it.

## The partner's position: contribution without equity

A specific injustice founder partners often feel and rarely say: they contributed to the company's existence without receiving any of its equity.

They supported the founder through the unpaid years. They took the stable job, or stepped back from their career, or ran the household so the founder could work 80 hours. They absorbed the risk of failure without the upside of success.

This contribution is real, and in most legal frameworks it is recognised only indirectly, through marital property rules that vary and that the prenup may be designed to override.

Founders who understand this don't just protect the company. They proactively ask: what would make this fair to you, if it ended? And they put the answer in the agreement, not as a concession but as a recognition. Partners who feel recognised sign readily. Partners who feel managed sign resentfully, or not at all.

## Self-check: Where are you each coming from?

Answer separately, honestly.

1. Who raised this, and what did you hear when they did?
2. What are you most afraid of in this conversation? Say the actual fear.
3. Do you believe the company is yours, ours, or something in between? Does your partner know what you believe?
4. Founder: have you separated "protecting the company" from "what's fair to my partner"?
5. Partner: can you separate "they want the company protected" from "they're planning to leave"?
6. If a third party pushed for this, has the founder sat on your side of the table first?
7. What would make the agreement feel like an act of partnership rather than a hedge?
8. Have you each got your own lawyer, so neither of you is relying on the other's?

If you can't answer 3 for your partner, start there. That's the conversation.

## What doesn't help

**Leading with the lawyer.** The lawyer should implement an agreement you've reached, not mediate the one you haven't.

**"It's just standard."** Nothing about this is standard to the person being asked to sign it.

**Relaying third-party demands.** You are a couple before you are a counterparty.

**Protecting the company and ignoring fairness.** Communicates exactly what the partner fears.

**Refusing to discuss it at all.** Silence reads as either naivety or contempt, depending on who's refusing.

**Treating the partner's hurt as irrational.** It isn't. It's a response to being asked to plan for loss.

**One lawyer for both.** Legally risky and relationally lopsided.

## What helps: Having the conversation well

### 1. Name the fears first
Each of you says what you're actually afraid of. Not the position. The fear. Most of the heat goes out of the room.

### 2. Separate the two protections
Company from divorce. Partner from unfairness. Agree you want both. Design both.

### 3. State your beliefs about whose it is
Before any documents. Out loud. Then find the shared understanding the documents should express.

### 4. Founder: sit on the partner's side first
Especially when investors or co-founders are pushing. Decide what's fair between you, then face them together.

### 5. Recognise contribution explicitly
In words and, where appropriate, in the agreement. The partner's unpaid, un-equitied support is real.

### 6. Get two lawyers
One each. It protects both of you legally and signals that you're not asking the partner to trust your lawyer's version of fairness.

### 7. Own the timing
If it's late, say so. If it's awkward, say so. Owning it disarms it.

### 8. Revisit it
Agreements made before the company was worth anything may need revisiting when it is. Build in a review.

## Making the documents an act of partnership

A prenup or postnup can be one of two things.

It can be a hedge: a document that says, in effect, *I'm preparing for you to fail me.* These agreements get signed, and something in the marriage goes quiet.

Or it can be an act of partnership: a document that says, *we've thought carefully about what's fair to both of us, we've protected the people who depend on the company, and we've written down what we each believe so that nothing is ambiguous.* These agreements get signed and the marriage is stronger for the conversation.

Same legal instrument. Entirely different meaning. The difference is made in the conversation, not the drafting.

## FAQ: Prenups, equity and founder marriages

### Should founders have a prenup?

That's a legal and financial question for a qualified attorney in your jurisdiction, and the answer depends on your situation. The relational question is different: whatever you decide, have the conversation underneath it well, separating protection of the company from fairness to your partner, and naming what each of you fears. The document matters less than the conversation that produces it.

### Why does asking for a prenup feel like a betrayal?

Because it forces into the open two things couples prefer to leave implicit: what is yours versus ours, and what happens if this ends. The partner may hear "the most important thing in your life is not something we share" or "you're planning for this to fail." Those fears are legitimate responses, not irrationality, and they need to be spoken before any drafting.

### How do we talk about equity in a marriage without fighting?

Say what each of you actually believes about whose the company is, out loud, before lawyers are involved. The founder's building is real; the partner's holding of the life that made it possible is also real. Find the shared understanding first. Documents that impose one partner's belief on the other produce resentment; documents that express a shared understanding produce trust.

### What if investors want my spouse to sign a waiver?

Sit on your spouse's side of the table first. Agree between you what is fair, including fairness to your partner, and then handle the investors' request together as co-deciders. Relaying the demand as a business requirement makes your spouse an obstacle and you a messenger for strangers, and no document fixes what that communicates.

### Is a postnup different from a prenup, relationally?

Often harder. The partner has already invested years without any agreement and is being asked to formalise terms after the fact. Frame it as clarifying what you both believe rather than limiting the partner, acknowledge the timing openly, and ensure the agreement recognises the partner's contribution, not just the company's protection.

### How should the non-founder partner's contribution be recognised?

Explicitly, in words and where appropriate in the agreement. The partner's unpaid support, deferred career and absorbed risk are real contributions to the company's existence that equity doesn't capture. Founders who proactively ask "what would make this fair to you if it ended?" and build the answer in find partners who sign readily rather than resentfully.

### Can a couples therapist help with prenup conversations?

Not with the terms; that's for lawyers. But when the conversation has stalled, become a fight about trust, or left one partner feeling managed or suspected, a therapist helps each of you hear the other's actual fear, separate the company from the marriage, and arrive at the lawyers as partners rather than opponents.
