Most couples don't recognise their own fights. They remember the topic (the dishes, the money, the in-laws) and they remember how it felt (awful), but they can't see the moves. One of you says something, the other does something, and ninety seconds later you're somewhere neither of you wanted to be.

The Four Horsemen is a way of seeing the moves. It names four specific things people do in conflict that make it worse, and it turns out to be one of the most useful vocabularies couples ever learn, because once you can name the move, you can stop making it.

## Short answer: What are the Four Horsemen?

Four conflict behaviours that research has repeatedly associated with relationships that fail:

1. **Criticism**: attacking your partner's character or personality instead of describing a specific behaviour and its effect on you.
2. **Contempt**: communicating disgust or superiority through sarcasm, mockery, eye-rolling, name-calling or sneering.
3. **Defensiveness**: responding to a complaint by counter-attacking, making excuses or playing the victim, so the complaint is never heard.
4. **Stonewalling**: withdrawing from the interaction, going silent, shutting down, physically or emotionally leaving.

Each has a replacement behaviour, and the point of learning the horsemen is to catch yourself mid-move and substitute it.

## Where the idea comes from

The framework emerged from decades of laboratory observation of couples in conflict. Researchers coded what partners actually did, moment by moment, during disagreements, and tracked which couples were still together years later.

Four behaviours stood out as strongly associated with later separation. The researchers gave them a name borrowed from the Book of Revelation, and it stuck.

The horsemen are not the only predictors the research identified, and the prediction accuracy figures that circulate online have been questioned by other researchers for methodological reasons. But the core finding, that these four behaviours are destructive and that reducing them helps, is well supported and widely accepted across the couples-therapy field, including by therapists who don't practise the Gottman Method. ## Horseman 1: Criticism

**What it is.** A complaint is about a specific thing: "You didn't call when you said you would, and I was worried." Criticism is about the person: "You never think about anyone but yourself." The shift from behaviour to character is the whole difference.

**What it sounds like.**
- "You always…" / "You never…"
- "What is wrong with you?"
- "You're so selfish / lazy / cold / irresponsible."
- "Why can't you just be normal about this?"

**Why it does damage.** Character attacks can't be answered. A partner can change a behaviour; they can't change who they are on the spot. So criticism produces either defensiveness or counter-criticism, and the specific complaint underneath never gets addressed.

**Why people do it.** Usually because the specific complaint has been made before and ignored, and criticism feels like turning up the volume. Or because the frustration has been accumulating and comes out as a verdict.

**The antidote: a gentle start-up.** Describe the situation, say what you feel, say what you need. "When you didn't call, I felt scared, and I need to know if plans change." No character verdict. The research finding behind this: how a conversation starts predicts how it ends with remarkable reliability. A harsh start almost never recovers.

## Horseman 2: Contempt

**What it is.** Criticism from a position of superiority. Not "you did something wrong" but "you are beneath me." It's communicated as much by tone and face as by words.

**What it looks and sounds like.**
- Eye-rolling, sneering, a particular kind of laugh
- Sarcasm: "Oh, you remembered. Congratulations."
- Mockery, mimicking, name-calling
- "You're pathetic." / "Of course you'd think that."
- Correcting or lecturing as though the partner were a child
- Hostile humour that only one of you finds funny

**Why it does damage.** Contempt communicates disgust, and disgust is incompatible with love. A partner on the receiving end of contempt feels worthless and unsafe, and no repair is possible while the contempt is in the room. It is also the horseman most associated with physical health effects in the partner receiving it: chronic contempt is corrosive in a measurable way.

**Why people do it.** Contempt grows from long-simmering negative thoughts about the partner. It's what criticism becomes when it's been running for years and the fondness underneath has drained away.

**The antidote: a culture of appreciation.** Contempt can't be fixed in the moment the way a harsh start-up can. It's prevented by rebuilding the habit of noticing what you value about your partner and saying it. Couples who maintain a strong current of appreciation and respect don't have the ground for contempt to grow in. If contempt is already established, the work is deeper, and it usually needs help.

## Horseman 3: Defensiveness

**What it is.** Responding to a complaint, fair or not, by refusing to receive it. Counter-attacking, making excuses, denying, or casting yourself as the victim of the complaint.

**What it sounds like.**
- "Well, you didn't do X either."
- "It's not my fault, I was busy."
- "I was just trying to help and this is what I get."
- "Why are you always attacking me?"
- "That's not what happened."

**Why it does damage.** Defensiveness says, in effect, "the problem isn't me, it's you." The partner who raised the complaint feels unheard and escalates, usually into criticism. Nothing gets resolved, and both partners leave feeling attacked.

**Why people do it.** Because being criticised feels like being attacked, and defending is the instinctive response. The more criticism there's been, the more automatic the defensiveness. The two feed each other.

**The antidote: take responsibility for some part of it.** Not full capitulation. Just: find the piece of the complaint that's true, and own it. "You're right, I did say I'd call. I should have." This small act stops the escalation almost immediately, because the complaining partner has been heard. The rest of the conversation can then happen.

## Horseman 4: Stonewalling

**What it is.** Withdrawing from the conflict. Going silent, looking away, giving one-word answers, leaving the room, becoming a wall. Not the same as a deliberate pause; stonewalling is a shutdown.

**What it looks like.**
- Silence while the other partner talks
- Looking at the floor, the phone, the wall
- "Fine." / "Whatever." / "I don't want to talk about this."
- Physically leaving mid-conversation without saying when you'll return
- Being present but completely unreachable

**Why it does damage.** The partner facing a stonewall experiences abandonment. They escalate to break through, which produces more stonewalling. Over time, the stonewalling partner is read as not caring, when the reality is usually the opposite.

**Why people do it.** Physiological flooding. The research finding here is specific: during conflict, heart rate rises, stress hormones spike, and above a certain threshold a person loses the capacity to process what's being said or respond constructively. Stonewalling is often what flooding looks like from the outside. The stonewaller isn't choosing to withdraw; they've gone offline.

**The antidote: physiological self-soothing, and a return.** Recognise the flooding. Say so: "I'm overwhelmed, I need twenty minutes." Take a real break, at least twenty minutes, doing something that calms rather than something that rehearses the argument. Then come back. The return is essential; a break without a return is just stonewalling with notice.

## Why contempt is the one to watch

Of the four, contempt is consistently identified as the strongest single predictor of relationship failure, and it's worth understanding why.

Criticism, defensiveness and stonewalling are all, in a sense, about the conflict. They're bad ways of handling a disagreement. Contempt is about the person. It says: I don't respect you. It can appear in the absence of any specific disagreement, as a tone that's simply there.

And it's the hardest to reverse, because its antidote isn't a technique you can apply in the moment. It's the slow rebuilding of fondness and respect. Couples where contempt has become habitual usually need help, because the appreciation that would counteract it has been eroded by the same process that produced the contempt.

If you recognise contempt in your relationship, in either direction, take it seriously. It's the horseman that most often signals the relationship is in real trouble.

## How the horsemen chain together

The horsemen rarely appear alone. They sequence.

A typical chain: Partner A has a complaint that's gone unaddressed. They raise it as **criticism** ("you never…"). Partner B hears an attack and goes **defensive** ("well, you…"). A, unheard, escalates, and the frustration tips into **contempt** (an eye-roll, a sneer). B, now flooded, **stonewalls**. A, facing a wall, escalates further. B shuts down completely or leaves.

Both partners can describe this sequence once they've seen it. Most couples can also identify where they typically enter it and which horseman is "theirs." Knowing your own entry point is the most practical thing to take from the framework: that's the move you catch.

## Self-check: Which horsemen ride in your relationship?

Answer separately, then compare. Be honest about your own, not your partner's.

**Criticism**
1. Do you use "always" or "never" when raising a problem?
2. Do your complaints tend to be about what your partner *did* or about who they *are*?

**Contempt**
3. Do you roll your eyes, sigh pointedly or use a sarcastic tone with your partner?
4. Do you ever think of your partner as stupid, pathetic or beneath you, even briefly?
5. Does your partner ever say you talk to them like a child?

**Defensiveness**
6. When your partner raises something, is your first response to explain why it wasn't your fault?
7. Do you find yourself saying "well, you…" in arguments?

**Stonewalling**
8. Do you go silent, leave, or shut down in conflict?
9. If you take a break, do you come back, or does the break end the conversation?

**Scoring:** Two or more yeses in any one horseman means it's active in your relationship. Yeses in contempt (3–5) warrant particular attention. If both partners score across all four, the chain is running regularly.

## The horsemen and the cycle underneath them

The Four Horsemen describe *what you do* in conflict. They're behaviours, and they're observable. This is their strength as a tool: you can catch them.

They don't describe *why*. Underneath the horsemen, in almost every couple, is a cycle: a pattern of pursuit and withdrawal, or mutual attack, or mutual avoidance, that runs regardless of topic, and that each partner's horsemen are serving. The critic is usually pursuing: trying to get a response from a partner they experience as unreachable. The stonewaller is usually withdrawing: protecting themselves from a partner they experience as attacking. Each horseman is a move in the cycle.

This matters because the antidotes work best when the cycle is also understood. A partner who learns to replace criticism with gentle start-ups, but who is still fundamentally terrified that their partner doesn't care, will slip back under pressure. A partner who learns to take breaks instead of stonewalling, but who still experiences every complaint as proof they're failing, will find the breaks getting longer.

We cover the cycle itself, pursue–withdraw, attack–attack, withdraw–withdraw, and how to break it, in *Same fight over and over: four conflict patterns*. The horsemen are the moves; the cycle is the game. You need to see both.

## When the antidotes don't hold

Many couples learn the horsemen, practise the antidotes, and improve. Some learn them, practise them, and find the horsemen keep coming back. If that's you, it usually means one of three things:

**The cycle underneath is strong.** The antidotes are being applied on top of an attachment pattern that hasn't been touched. This is where therapy that works with the cycle, rather than only with the behaviours, tends to be needed.

**Contempt has taken root.** Once contempt is habitual, behavioural antidotes alone rarely reverse it. The fondness underneath has to be rebuilt, and that's slow work that benefits from a third person in the room.

**One partner is applying the antidotes and the other isn't.** The framework assumes both are trying. If one partner has learned the vocabulary and uses it to score points ("that was contempt, by the way"), it becomes another weapon.

None of these means the framework failed. It means the framework has shown you where the deeper work is.

## The horsemen in high-pressure couples

A note for couples where one or both partners carry demanding professional roles, because the horsemen show up in particular ways.

**Criticism becomes evaluation.** High achievers often don't say "you're lazy." They say "that wasn't well handled," in the tone they'd use with a report. It's criticism in professional dress, and the partner feels it as a performance review.

**Contempt becomes condescension.** Not sneering; explaining. Lecturing a partner on how something should have been done. It reads as contempt because it is.

**Defensiveness becomes rebuttal.** Articulate, structured, point-by-point. The partner's complaint is defeated rather than heard. The high achiever wins the argument and loses the relationship.

**Stonewalling becomes the laptop.** Withdrawal into work, which looks like diligence and functions as a wall.

## FAQ: The Four Horsemen

### What are the Four Horsemen in a relationship?

Four conflict behaviours that research has linked to relationship failure: criticism (attacking character rather than describing a specific behaviour), contempt (disrespect, mockery, superiority), defensiveness (deflecting a complaint instead of hearing it) and stonewalling (shutting down and withdrawing). Each has a replacement behaviour that interrupts the damage.

### What are the antidotes to the Four Horsemen?

Criticism → a gentle start-up (describe the situation, say what you feel, say what you need). Contempt → a culture of appreciation (actively building fondness and respect so contempt has no ground). Defensiveness → taking responsibility for the part of the complaint that's true. Stonewalling → recognising flooding, taking a real break of at least twenty minutes, and returning.

### Why is contempt the worst of the Four Horsemen?

Because it's about the person, not the conflict: it communicates disgust and superiority, which are incompatible with love and make repair impossible while present. It's the strongest single predictor of separation in the research and the hardest to reverse, since its antidote isn't a technique but the slow rebuilding of fondness that the contempt itself has eroded.

### What's the difference between a complaint and criticism?

A complaint is about a specific behaviour and its effect: "You didn't call, and I was worried." Criticism is about character: "You never think about anyone else." Complaints can be heard and acted on. Criticism produces defensiveness or counter-attack, and the original issue never gets addressed.

### Is stonewalling the same as taking a break?

No. A break is announced ("I'm overwhelmed, I need twenty minutes"), has a defined length, is spent calming down rather than rehearsing the argument, and ends with a return to the conversation. Stonewalling is an unannounced shutdown with no return. The return is what makes the difference.

### Can the Four Horsemen be fixed without therapy?

Often, yes, if both partners learn the framework, identify their own horseman, and practise the antidotes consistently. They tend not to hold without help when contempt has become habitual, when the attachment cycle underneath is strong, or when only one partner is doing the work. In those cases the framework has shown you where the deeper work is.

### Do I need a Gottman therapist to work on the Four Horsemen?

No. The horsemen are widely used across couples therapy regardless of a therapist's primary model, because they're a clear vocabulary for observable conflict behaviours. EFT-based therapists, including Aether Practice, use them alongside work on the cycle underneath. If you specifically want Gottman Method therapy, look for a certified Gottman therapist.
