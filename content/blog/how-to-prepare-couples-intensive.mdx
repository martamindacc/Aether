You've booked. Or you're about to. And now you're looking at a date three weeks away and wondering whether you should be doing something.

You should, but less than you think, and not the things you think. Most couples prepare for an intensive the way they'd prepare for a difficult meeting: by building their case. That's the one thing that doesn't help. This is what does.

## Short answer: What do we actually need to do?

**Logistically:** clear both days and the evening between them completely, arrange care for children and pets, tell work you're unreachable, and decide where you'll stay.

**Individually:** complete the written preparation the practice sends, separately and honestly, without comparing notes.

**Together:** agree one thing in advance: how you'll handle the evening between the two days. Then stop. Don't rehearse, don't pre-litigate, don't try to solve it before you arrive.

**Internally:** arrive rested, fed and as un-defended as you can manage. The intensive will do the rest.

## Why preparation changes the outcome

An intensive is 12–14 hours of concentrated work. The quality of those hours depends on things that are decided before you walk in.

If one of you is checking a phone, half the room is gone. If you fought in the car on the way, the first block is spent recovering. If one of you came to make a case and the other to defend against it, the therapist spends day one dismantling positions instead of mapping the pattern. If neither of you has thought about what you want to be different, day two has nowhere to land.

None of this is fatal. All of it is avoidable.

## Three weeks out: Decisions and logistics

**Confirm the format and dates.** Two consecutive days is standard; see [the hub](/intensive-couples-therapy) for alternatives. Choose dates with nothing heavy on either side, ideally with a lighter day after.

**Choose the room.** The intensive is online, so identify the room now: private, comfortable, both of you in it together, with a door that closes and a stable connection.

**Decide where you'll sleep.** Home is fine if home is calm. Many couples choose a hotel or rental, not as a treat but to be out of the environment where the pattern lives. If you have children, somewhere else is often the only way to actually get the evening.

**Book care.** Children, pets, anyone who depends on you, for both full days and the evening and night between. Not "my mother can probably." Confirmed.

**Block work.** Both calendars. Out-of-office on. If you lead a team, delegate explicitly. The intensive fails if one of you is mentally in a deal.

**Tell the practice about anything relevant.** Medications, mental health history, a recent event, travel the day before. They'd rather know.

## Two weeks out: The individual preparation

Most intensives include written preparation for each partner, completed separately. Ours covers the ground below; other practices vary, but the principles are the same.

**Do it alone.** Not in the same room, not on the same evening, not compared afterwards. The therapist needs each of your honest pictures, and honesty bends when you know your partner will read it.

**Be specific.** "Communication" is not a problem; "when I bring up money, he goes quiet and leaves the room, and then I follow him and it escalates" is a problem the therapist can work with.

**Typical questions:**
- How did you meet, and what was the relationship like at its best?
- When did things start to change? What did you notice first?
- Describe the last significant argument: what started it, what each of you did, how it ended.
- What do you think your partner would say the problem is?
- What's your own part in the pattern? (The hardest question. Answer it anyway.)
- Is there anything your partner doesn't know that affects the relationship?
- What do you want to be different after the intensive? Concretely.
- On a scale, how committed are you to this relationship right now? Be honest; the number is confidential and it helps the therapist calibrate.
- Is there anything you're afraid will happen in the room?

**The last two questions matter most.** Knowing what you want to be different gives day two a destination. Naming what you're afraid of tells the therapist where to go carefully.

**Return it on time.** The therapist reads both before you arrive and shapes the first morning around them.

## One week out: Protecting the days

**Don't schedule anything emotionally heavy** in the week before: no major family events, no big conversations you've been putting off, no "let's just quickly talk about" the thing you'll talk about in the intensive.

**Reduce load where you can.** Lighter work week if possible. The intensive is tiring; arriving already depleted costs you day one.

**Sleep.** Prioritise it. Sleep-deprived people flood faster and recover slower, and the intensive asks a lot of both.

**Reconfirm care and logistics.** Once. Then trust it.

**Agree the one thing.** How you'll handle the evening between the days. See the section below. This is the only joint preparation you should do.

## What to tell work, children and family

**Work.** As much or as little as you like. "I'm taking two days off and will be unreachable" is complete. If you lead a team, make sure the people who'd normally reach you know who to reach instead. The test: could a crisis happen without you hearing about it until Monday? If not, you haven't cleared it.

**Children.** Depends on age. For young children: "Mum and Dad are going to spend two days working on being a good team, and Grandma's going to have fun with you." For older children: they usually already know something is off, and a plain "we're spending a couple of days with someone who helps couples get better at being together" is more reassuring than secrecy. Don't say "fixing our marriage"; it frightens them.

**Family and friends.** Only what you want to. Many couples tell no one. Some tell one trusted person so there's someone to debrief with afterwards who isn't the partner. If a family member is providing childcare, they need to know you're unreachable, not why.

## The night before: Do and don't

**Do**
- Eat a real dinner
- Go to bed early
- Pack (see below)
- Say one kind thing to each other
- Agree what time you'll leave in the morning
- Phones charged, then put away

**Don't**
- Have the argument. If it starts, one of you says "we have a room for this tomorrow," and you stop.
- Drink more than one. Alcohol disrupts sleep and lowers the threshold for the argument.
- Review your preparation answers or your partner's
- Build your case. If you catch yourself rehearsing what you'll say, notice it and stop. The intensive is not a hearing.
- Stay up late "getting things done." They'll be there Monday.
- Discuss expectations in detail. "I hope it helps" is enough. "Here's what I need you to admit" is not.

## What to bring

- Water. Both days.
- Snacks that don't need preparation, for breaks. Blood sugar matters more than you'd think across six hours.
- Layers. Rooms run cold or hot and you'll be sitting for long stretches.
- Tissues, if the practice doesn't provide them. They will. Bring some anyway.
- A notebook each, for the agreements on day two and for anything you want to remember. Not for taking notes on your partner.
- Anything medically necessary.
- Nothing else. Not a laptop. Not work reading for the breaks. If online, the device for the session and nothing open on it but the session.

## The evening between day one and day two

This is the one piece of joint preparation worth doing in advance, because it's where intensives most often get undone.

Day one is exhausting. You will have heard things. One of you may be raw; the other may be flat. The temptation is to continue: to debrief, to process, to finish the conversation the therapist interrupted, to ask the question you didn't ask in the room.

Don't. Agree now, before the intensive, on these rules for the evening:

1. **No continuing the work.** Anything that came up goes to tomorrow's room, not tonight's kitchen.
2. **No analysing each other.** "I noticed when you said…" is work. Save it.
3. **Low demands.** Simple food, something undemanding to watch or read, an early night.
4. **One kind thing.** A hand on the back. "Thank you for today." Nothing more required.
5. **Permission to be separate.** If one of you needs a walk alone, that's fine and not a withdrawal. Say "I need half an hour," and take it.
6. **The phrase.** Agree a sentence either of you can use to stop a conversation that's drifting into the work: "Tomorrow." That's enough.

Couples who agree this in advance arrive on day two with something left. Couples who don't often arrive having had the fight at midnight.

## Arriving in the right state

Not relaxed. You won't be, and the therapist doesn't expect it. The state you're aiming for is something more like *open and tired*, rather than *composed and ready*.

Specifically:

- **Fed and hydrated.** Low blood sugar looks a lot like irritability.
- **Not mid-conflict.** If the drive goes wrong, sit in the car for five minutes before going in. Tell the therapist the drive went badly; it's useful information and it stops the first block being a recovery.
- **Phones off**, not silent. Off.
- **Without a case.** You're not there to prove anything. If you notice you've prepared an argument, let it go. The therapist will find the pattern without your brief.
- **Expecting to be uncomfortable.** It's the point. Discomfort in the room is cheaper than discomfort at home.

## Preparing for after: The fragile week

Most couples underprepare for the week after, and it's where gains are most easily lost.

**Expect:** closeness and fragility together. Tiredness that lasts days. At least one return to the old pattern, often by Thursday. A feeling, in one or both of you, of "did that even happen."

**Plan now:**
- A lighter first day back. Not a board meeting on Monday morning.
- The first follow-up booked before you leave day two, ideally within seven to ten days.
- One agreed thing you'll each do daily for the first week, from the day-two agreements. Small. Kept.
- Who you'll each talk to if it gets hard and the other isn't the right person in that moment.
- The reminder, written down somewhere you'll see it: *the relapse is expected. The follow-up is where we work it.*

## If you're not ready

Preparation sometimes reveals that the timing is wrong. Signs:

- One of you is doing the preparation to appease the other, not because you want to be there
- Something significant has just happened and you're still in shock
- You can't clear the days without one of you being mentally elsewhere
- The individual preparation surfaces something undisclosed that needs to be dealt with first
- Either of you is in a period of acute depression, anxiety or substance use that would make two days of this unsafe

None of these means never. They mean not this date. Tell the practice; a good one will reschedule without drama and may suggest a different route in the meantime. See [Intensive vs weekly](/blog/intensive-vs-weekly-couples-therapy) for the alternatives.

## FAQ: Preparing for a couples intensive

### How should we prepare for a couples therapy intensive?

Clear both days and the evening between them completely, arrange confirmed care for children and pets, complete the written preparation separately and honestly, agree in advance how you'll handle the night between the days, and arrive rested and fed rather than mid-argument. Don't build a case or rehearse; the intensive isn't a hearing.

### Should we talk about what we'll say before the intensive?

No. The only joint preparation worth doing is agreeing how you'll handle the evening between day one and day two. Pre-discussing the issues tends to either start the argument early or produce rehearsed positions that the therapist then has to dismantle. Do your individual preparation separately and bring the rest to the room.

### What do we tell our children about a couples intensive?

For young children, something simple: you're spending two days working on being a good team while they have time with a caregiver. For older children, who usually sense something anyway, a plain statement that you're spending time with someone who helps couples get better at being together is more reassuring than secrecy. Avoid "fixing our marriage."

### Should we stay at a hotel during a couples intensive?

Many couples do, not as a treat but to be out of the environment where the pattern lives and to actually get the evening to themselves. If home is calm and childcare is handled elsewhere, home works. The key is that the evening between the days is protected and low-demand.

### What should we not do the night before a couples intensive?

Don't have the argument (agree a stop phrase: "tomorrow"), don't drink more than one, don't review your preparation answers, don't build your case, and don't stay up late. Eat well, pack, say one kind thing, and sleep.

### What happens if we fight during the evening between the two days?

It happens, and it's recoverable. Tell the therapist first thing on day two; it's useful information and it stops the first block being spent recovering. To prevent it: agree the evening rules in advance (no continuing the work, no analysing, low demands, one kind thing, permission to be separate, a stop phrase).

### How do we prepare for the week after an intensive?

Expect closeness and fragility together, tiredness, and at least one return to the old pattern. Plan a lighter first day back, book the first follow-up before leaving day two, pick one small daily action each from the agreements, and write down somewhere visible that the relapse is expected and the follow-up is where you work it.
