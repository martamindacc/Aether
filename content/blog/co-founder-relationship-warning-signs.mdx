*This article is part of our guide to [co-founder conflict](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide).*

Founders are good at reading markets, hires and investors. They are strikingly bad at reading the relationship the company depends on, mostly because the signals are small, each one is explicable on its own, and raising any of them feels disproportionate.

So here they are, ranked. Not so you can diagnose your co-founder, but so you can notice what you have been explaining away.

## Short answer: how do you know a co-founder relationship is in trouble?

**A co-founder relationship is in trouble when disagreement has stopped producing decisions. The reliable signs are decisions that get reopened, a subject the two of you avoid, arguments prepared in advance, important things routed through other people, and a team that has started managing around the tension.** Volume is not a sign; loud founding teams can be very healthy. The question is whether a disagreement ends in a decision both of you execute, or in a truce that gets quietly relitigated.

If three or more of the twelve signs below are present, you have a pattern rather than a bad month, and patterns do not resolve by themselves.

## The twelve signs, ranked

1. You have stopped disagreeing in meetings.
2. A decision from last quarter is back on the agenda.
3. You know what the other person will say before they say it, and you have stopped asking.
4. The partnership has no slot in the calendar.
5. There is a subject the two of you have not raised in over a month.
6. You prepare arguments before conversations.
7. One of you keeps a private tally of who gave in.
8. Important things get routed through a third person.
9. The team has started managing around the tension.
10. You have talked to an investor, advisor or lawyer about your co-founder before talking to your co-founder.
11. You cannot describe a version of the partnership you would both choose.
12. One of you has already left, and is waiting for a date.

## Ordinary under load: signs 1 to 4

These show up in almost every founding team under pressure. Alone, they are maintenance items. Together, they are the beginning of something.

### 1. You have stopped disagreeing in meetings

**What it means.** Disagreement has moved somewhere else: the corridor, Slack, or nowhere. Two founders who always agree in the room are either one person with redundant payroll or two people who have stopped saying what they think.

**What to do.** Say one true disagreement out loud in the next meeting, about something small. The point is to find out whether the room can still hold it.

### 2. A decision from last quarter is back on the agenda

**What it means.** It was never actually decided. One of you stopped arguing, and the other mistook silence for agreement. This is the most visible symptom of [a decision-rights problem](/blog/co-founder-5050-deadlock), and it compounds: every reopened decision teaches both of you that nothing is ever settled.

**What to do.** Ask whose call it was. If the answer is "both of ours", that is the problem, and it is fixable with domain ownership.

### 3. You know what the other person will say, and you have stopped asking

**What it means.** You have replaced your co-founder with a model of your co-founder. The model is probably two years out of date, and it is certainly less generous than the person.

**What to do.** Ask anyway, once, and listen for the thing the model would not have predicted.

### 4. The partnership has no slot in the calendar

**What it means.** You have four weekly meetings about the company and none about the thing the company depends on. Nothing is wrong yet. Nothing is being maintained either.

**What to do.** A standing hour, outside the operating cadence, where the only agenda is the two of you. Y Combinator's partners and the coaches who work with its companies give the same advice; it is the cheapest process a founding team can install.

## A pattern, not a mood: signs 5 to 8

These do not appear in healthy teams under load. They appear when something has been unresolved long enough to change how the two of you operate.

### 5. There is a subject the two of you have not raised in over a month

**What it means.** The avoided subject. Usually equity, someone's performance, or whether one of you still wants to be here. Everything else in the relationship has got slightly more careful, because every conversation now has to navigate around the thing in the middle of it.

**What to do.** Name that the subject exists before trying to resolve it. "There is something we are not talking about, and I would like to" is a complete first move.

### 6. You prepare arguments before conversations

**What it means.** Conversations have become negotiations. You are no longer thinking together; you are presenting positions. This is the point at which founders start to describe the relationship as "exhausting" without being able to say why.

**What to do.** Write your position down and send it before the conversation, and ask your co-founder to do the same. Written positions are harder to escalate and much harder to misremember.

### 7. One of you keeps a private tally of who gave in

**What it means.** The contribution ledger, or its cousin the concession ledger. It is private, so it never gets corrected, and both founders usually believe they are the one carrying more. It is the single most common thing underneath the sentence "I just feel like I'm doing everything."

**What to do.** Say that there is a ledger, and what is on it, before it comes out during an argument about something minor as a list going back years. If the ledger is about effort, [this is the conversation](/blog/co-founder-not-pulling-weight). If it is about ownership, [this one](/blog/renegotiating-co-founder-equity).

### 8. Important things get routed through a third person

**What it means.** One of you has started looping in a senior hire, an advisor or a board member rather than raising something directly. It reads as process. It functions as avoidance, and the third person has now been recruited into a dynamic they did not ask to join.

**What to do.** Notice who you told first, and tell your co-founder second, today.

## Running on avoidance: signs 9 to 12

At this stage the partnership may still be repairable, and many are. But not through the normal channels, and not without someone outside the two of you in the room.

### 9. The team has started managing around the tension

**What it means.** They noticed long before you raised it. They now know which founder to ask for what, which topics not to put in a shared channel, and which of you actually runs the company. That is a culture cost separate from the partnership cost, and it is already being paid.

**What to do.** Stop performing unity in front of the team. They can see through it, and the performance costs you more credibility than the disagreement would.

### 10. You have talked to an investor, advisor or lawyer about your co-founder before talking to your co-founder

**What it means.** You have already made a decision about the partnership, even if you have not admitted it to yourself. The conflict now has a constituency, and your co-founder will eventually learn it was discussed without them, which ends most chances of repair. [Each of those people solves a different problem](/blog/co-founder-mediation-vs-coaching), and none of them is the place to find out what is happening between the two of you.

**What to do.** Tell your co-founder you have been struggling with the partnership, before anyone else tells them for you.

### 11. You cannot describe a version of the partnership you would both choose

**What it means.** This is the honest test of whether a partnership is finished. If both of you can describe a version, even roughly, you are not done. If one of you cannot, you are, and [separating well](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit) is now the skill that matters.

**What to do.** Ask the question directly, and ask it with someone present who can hold both answers at once.

### 12. One of you has already left, and is waiting for a date

**What it means.** Not resigned. Left. The work is minimal, the enthusiasm is elsewhere, and the only thing holding them is a vesting date or the awkwardness of saying it. This partnership ended some time ago; what remains is the administration, and the administration goes far better when it is named.

**What to do.** If it is you, say so, and manage your own exit well. If it is them, ask, in a way that assumes you do not already know the answer.

## Signs that are not warning signs

Worth listing, because founders misread these constantly.

**Loud disagreement.** Two people who argue hard on Tuesday and ship on Wednesday are doing what a founding pair is for.

**Different working hours.** Hours are the worst available proxy for contribution and the most tempting one.

**A bad quarter.** Everyone has one. The question is whether the two of you decided things during it.

**Not being friends.** Some of the most durable founding partnerships are between people who respect each other and would never holiday together. Friendship is not the asset; the ability to decide is.

## How to score yourself

Count the signs that are present, honestly, and then look at where they sit.

**Zero to two, all from the first group.** Ordinary stretch under load. Put the partnership in the calendar and move on.

**Three to five, or any from the second group.** A pattern, not a mood. It will not resolve by itself, and this is the point where a structured conversation is cheap and effective.

**Six or more, or any from the third group.** The partnership is operating on avoidance. It may well still be repairable, but not through the normal channels. [The eight-question Repairability Check in our complete guide](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide) is the next thing to do, and doing it with someone outside the two of you present is the thing after that.

## What to do about it

The temptation is to pick the most visible sign and fix it. Resist that; the signs are symptoms, and most of them trace back to the same two or three causes. Underneath nearly all twelve is some combination of unclear decision rights, an unspoken ledger, and a subject neither of you will raise.

So the useful sequence is: name that there is a pattern, not a complaint; put the partnership in the calendar; write down who decides what; and say the thing you have been not saying. [Our complete guide to co-founder conflict](/blog/co-founder-conflict-guide) walks through all of that in detail. ## FAQ: co-founder relationship warning signs

### What are the signs of a bad co-founder relationship?

The reliable ones are structural rather than emotional: decisions that get reopened, a subject the two of you avoid, arguments prepared in advance, important things routed through a third person, a private tally of who gave in, and a team that has started managing around the tension. Loud disagreement by itself is not a sign; disagreement that stops producing decisions is.

### Is it normal to argue with your co-founder?

Yes. Disagreement is what a founding pair is for; two people who always agree are one person with redundant payroll. What is not normal is disagreement that ends in a truce rather than a decision, or that has moved out of the room into Slack, the corridor or silence.

### What are co-founder red flags before you start a company?

Before starting, the strongest red flags are divergent ambition (one wants a venture outcome, the other a profitable company they own), refusal to discuss vesting or decision rights, a vague answer about how much time they can commit, and a history of partnerships that ended badly with the other person always at fault. [Thirty questions to settle before you start are here.](/blog/co-founder-agreement-questions)

### How do you know when a co-founder relationship is over?

Under two conditions, usually only two: what you want from the company has genuinely diverged and no structure reconciles it, or trust has been broken by a specific event neither of you can rebuild. Almost everything else, including a year of resentment, is repairable. [The honest test is whether both of you can describe a version of the partnership you would choose.](/blog/co-founder-breakup-exit)

### Can a co-founder relationship be saved?

Often, yes, particularly when the trouble is structural: deadlock, role drift, an unspoken ledger. The partnerships that do not recover are usually those where ambition has genuinely diverged or trust was broken by something specific and large. The earlier the signs are named, the more likely repair is.

### Should I tell my investors about problems with my co-founder?

Not as a first step, and not before you have talked to your co-founder. Investors are not neutral and cannot be; their obligation runs to the company. Work the problem privately first, and bring the board a resolution rather than a conflict.
