The question couples ask most before an intensive is some version of: *what are we going to do for fourteen hours?* Underneath it: *is it going to be fourteen hours of fighting?*

No. Most of it isn't fighting at all. It's slower and stranger than that, and knowing the shape in advance makes it far less frightening. This is what two days actually look like.

*This article is part of our hub on [intensive couples therapy](/intensive-couples-therapy).*

## Short answer: What actually happens?

**Day one is about seeing.** You tell the story, the therapist listens for the pattern, and by mid-afternoon you can both describe the cycle you get stuck in from the outside, as a thing that happens to you rather than something one of you does to the other.

**Day two is about reaching.** Once the pattern is visible, the work goes underneath it, to what each of you is protecting, and then one partner says something real and the other hears it, often for the first time in years. Then you agree what to take home.

Follow-up sessions in the weeks after anchor it.

## The shape of two days

| | Morning (9:00–12:30) | Afternoon (13:30–16:00) |
|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Arrival, your story, how it started, what changed | Mapping the cycle; naming it as shared |
| **Day 2** | The dip; going beneath the cycle | The turn; reaching; agreements for home |

Each half-day holds two 90-minute blocks with a 20–30 minute break between them, and a 60-minute lunch. That's the standard EFT-informed structure we use; Gottman-style intensives have a similar rhythm with more assessment up front.

## Before you arrive

You've had a consultation. You've each completed a short written preparation separately. The therapist has read both and has a hypothesis, which they will hold loosely.

You've arranged the practical things: childcare for both days and the evening, no work, no travel, phones off during blocks. [How to prepare for a couples intensive](/blog/how-to-prepare-couples-intensive) covers all of that; this article is about the room.

## Day one, morning: Arrival and mapping

**9:00–9:15 · Settling.** Coffee, the room, the rules: no interrupting during someone's turn, the therapist will stop you if it gets destructive, breaks are real, you can ask for one. Most couples are nervous. That's expected and said out loud.

**9:15–10:45 · Block 1: Your story.** Not the problem yet. How you met. What drew you to each other. What the relationship was like at its best. When things started to change, and what each of you noticed first. The therapist asks each of you in turn, and asks the other to just listen.

What the therapist is doing: watching how you talk about each other, who softens when, where the warmth still lives, and how each of you tells the story of the decline. Those differences are the first map.

What couples feel: often, unexpectedly, some tenderness. Many haven't told the beginning of their story in years.

**10:45–11:15 · Break.** Separately or together. No debriefing the block.

**11:15–12:45 · Block 2: The problem, from both sides.** Now the hard part. Each of you describes what you think is wrong, what you've tried, what the worst recent moment was. The therapist slows it down, asks for specifics, and starts tracking the sequence: who does what, then what, then what.

What the therapist is doing: looking for the cycle. Almost every distressed couple has one: a repeating sequence of moves (criticise, defend, withdraw, pursue) that runs regardless of topic. By the end of this block the therapist usually has it.

What couples feel: this is where fighting can start. The therapist's job is to catch it early, and to use the moment, because the cycle has just shown itself in the room.

**12:45–13:45 · Lunch.** Together is fine. Don't try to finish the argument.

## Day one, midday: Individual time

Within or around the lunch break, most intensives include 20–30 minutes with each partner alone. This is where the therapist asks what you couldn't say in front of your partner: what you're most afraid of, whether there's anything the other doesn't know, whether you're actually here to repair or to be sure before leaving.

Confidentiality is explained up front. The therapist won't disclose what you say, but will use it to understand what's happening, and will encourage you to bring it into the room yourself when you're ready.

## Day one, afternoon: The cycle

**13:45–15:15 · Block 3: Naming the cycle.** The therapist lays out the pattern they've seen: "When you feel X, you tend to do Y. When you see Y, you feel A and do B. And B lands on your partner as more X." Each of you is asked whether it fits, and to correct it. The goal is a description you both recognise.

Then the reframe that defines EFT-based work: *the cycle is the enemy, not your partner.* You are both caught in something. Neither of you designed it. Both of you keep it running.

What couples feel: relief, frequently. Something that felt like a character flaw in one of you becomes a mechanism between you. Some couples name the cycle; it makes it easier to talk about.

**15:15–15:30 · Break.**

**15:30–16:00 · Block 4 (short): Landing the day.** What did each of you learn? What surprised you? The therapist gives one instruction for the evening (below) and tells you what day two is for. No homework beyond that.

## Day one, evening: What to do and not do

**Do:** Rest. Eat well. Be kind to each other in small ways. Sleep.

**Don't:** Re-run the day. Finish the argument. Analyse your partner. Drink much. Check email. Make decisions.

The instruction most therapists give: *tonight, you are allowed to be two tired people who did something hard together. That's all.*

Day one is often exhausting in a way couples don't anticipate. That's not a sign it went badly.

## Day two, morning: The dip, and going underneath

**9:00–9:30 · Check-in.** How did you sleep? What came up overnight? Many couples arrive on day two flatter, more guarded, or unexpectedly raw. Therapists call this the day-two dip. It's normal: the defences that dropped on day one have had a night to notice they dropped. The therapist expects it and works with it.

**9:30–11:00 · Block 5: Beneath the cycle.** The work moves from what each of you *does* to what each of you *feels* underneath doing it. The withdrawing partner's shutting down, examined slowly, usually turns out to protect something: fear of failing, of not being enough, of making it worse. The pursuing partner's criticism, slowed down, usually protects fear of being alone, of not mattering, of being left.

The therapist works with one partner at a time while the other listens. The questions get quieter and more specific. "When she goes silent, what happens in your body?" "When he says that, what's the very first thing you feel, before the anger?"

What couples feel: this is the hardest block of the two days, and the one couples most often describe afterwards as the turning point. Partners hear fear where they'd heard attack. They hear longing where they'd heard indifference.

**11:00–11:30 · Break.** Take it.

**11:30–13:00 · Block 6: The other side.** The same work, with the other partner. The therapist is careful about order and balance; both partners need to be heard beneath the surface before anything is asked of either.

## Day two, midday: The turn

**13:00–14:00 · Lunch.** Often quieter than day one's. Something has shifted and neither of you is sure what to do with it yet.

## Day two, afternoon: Reaching, and what you take home

**14:00–15:30 · Block 7: Reaching.** This is the block the whole intensive has been building toward. With the therapist's help, one partner says the underneath thing *to* the other, directly: not "I feel unimportant" to the therapist, but "I need to know I matter to you" to their partner, looking at them. And the other partner responds, with the therapist helping them stay open rather than defend.

In EFT this is sometimes called a softening or a bonding event. It is not dramatic from the outside. From the inside, couples describe it as the first time they've reached each other in years.

Not every intensive gets a clean version of this on day two. Some get it in the follow-ups. The therapist won't force it; a forced reach is worse than none.

**15:30–15:45 · Break.**

**15:45–16:30 · Block 8: What you take home.** Practical, specific, written down. What is the cycle called, and how will you each recognise your first move into it? What's the signal to stop? What's the one thing each of you will do differently this week? When is the first follow-up? What do you do if it falls apart on Thursday?

The therapist also tells you what to expect in the next two weeks: a mix of closeness, fragility and tiredness, probably one return to the old cycle, and the follow-up as the place to work that.

## After: The follow-ups

Two to three sessions, usually 60 minutes, in the two to six weeks after. Their purpose is specific: the intensive happened in a room; the relationship lives in a kitchen. Follow-ups are where you report the Thursday it fell apart, see the cycle in it, and practise the repair. Research on intensive formats consistently points to follow-up as important for maintaining gains, and in our experience couples who skip them lose more than they expect.

## The emotional arc, honestly

Couples asking "what happens" usually also mean "how will it feel." Roughly:

- **Day one morning:** nervous, then unexpectedly warm.
- **Day one afternoon:** tense, then relieved when the cycle gets named.
- **Day one evening:** exhausted. Sometimes closer, sometimes flat.
- **Day two morning:** the dip. Guarded, raw, or "why are we doing this."
- **Day two midday:** something has moved; unsure what.
- **Day two afternoon:** the reach. Tender, often tearful, quiet.
- **The following week:** close, fragile, tired, at least one relapse.
- **After follow-ups:** steadier. The cycle still appears; now you can see it.

If your experience doesn't match this, that doesn't mean it's going wrong. It's a common shape, not a required one.

## Variations: one-day, three-day, online

**One-day intensives** compress the arc: mapping in the morning, beneath-the-cycle and a shorter reach in the afternoon. Best for couples with a specific issue or as a booster, not for deep repair.

**Three-day intensives** add a full day between mapping and reaching, usually for infidelity recovery, long-entrenched patterns, or where both partners need extended individual time. [See: Intensives for crisis.](/blog/couples-intensive-crisis-infidelity-separation)

**Online delivery.** Breaks matter more than they would in a room (screen fatigue is real), and the therapist will ask you to be in the same room as each other, not on separate screens.

For which format fits you, see [Intensive vs weekly](/blog/intensive-vs-weekly-couples-therapy).

## FAQ: What happens in a couples intensive

### How many hours a day is a couples intensive?

Typically six to seven hours of therapy per day, from around 9am to 4pm, in 90-minute blocks with 20–30 minute breaks and an hour for lunch. A two-day intensive totals about 12–14 hours of work; a three-day, 16–20.

### Will we just fight for two days?

No. Most of an intensive is slow and structured: each partner speaking while the other listens, the therapist tracking the pattern and slowing things down. Conflict does surface, and the therapist uses it to show the cycle in real time, but destructive escalation is interrupted. Most couples report less fighting in the intensive than at home.

### Do we meet the therapist separately?

Usually yes, for 20–30 minutes each on day one. This is where you can say what you couldn't in front of your partner. The therapist won't disclose it, but will use it to understand what's happening and will encourage you to bring it into the room yourself.

### What is the "day-two dip"?

A common experience where couples arrive on the second morning flatter, more guarded or unexpectedly raw than they left on day one. The defences that dropped have had a night to notice. Therapists expect it and work with it; it is not a sign the intensive is failing.

### What if we don't have a breakthrough?

Not every intensive produces a clean bonding moment on day two, and a good therapist won't force one. The work beneath the cycle still happens, the agreements still get made, and the reach often comes in the follow-ups. The intensive's job is to make the pattern visible and reachable; the breakthrough is one possible form of that.

### What happens after the intensive?

Two to three follow-up sessions over the following weeks, where you bring the moments it fell apart at home, see the cycle in them, and practise repair. Expect the first week to be a mix of closeness, fragility and tiredness, with at least one return to the old pattern. That's what the follow-ups are for.

### How does the intensive work online?

Same schedule, same structure, same follow-ups as described above. Breaks matter more because of screen fatigue, and you'll be asked to be physically together, not on separate devices. You need a private room where you can both speak freely for the full day.
